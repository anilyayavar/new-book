38  Multiple Regression in Audit

In Chapter 37 we explained the office expenses of block offices by one driver, their staff strength. That model found one of the two offices we had planted. It missed the other. It also pointed to several offices in the hills which may have done nothing wrong. One driver is rarely enough. The expenses of an office also depend on the beneficiaries it serves and on the terrain it covers.

An auditor meets this in most data. The cost of a road depends on its width, the terrain, the lead and the price of bitumen. The salary bill of an office depends on its staff and on the class of its city, which fixes the house rent allowance. Multiple regression lets us use all these drivers together. It gives a fairer expected amount for every unit. The units which spend more than expected then stand out more clearly, and innocent units drop away.

We end the chapter with the question we posed at the start of Chapter 37. Which offices spend much more on salary than their staff strength explains? That is where ghost employees hide.

In this chapter we will learn to

  • use several predictors together, including categories like terrain, and interactions,
  • detect predictors which repeat each other’s information (multi-collinearity),
  • avoid being misled by a hidden variable (Simpson’s paradox), and
  • finally, flag offices which spend more than expected, and measure the excess in rupees.

We use the same packages as in Chapter 37, and three more. car calculates the variance inflation factor. GGally draws correlation plots and moderndive draws parallel slopes.

38.2 The block office data

We continue with the office expenses of block offices, described in Section 37.2. For each office we know its district and terrain, its staff in position and sanctioned posts, the floor area of its building, the population of its block and the beneficiaries it serves. We also know its office expenses for 2023-24, in ₹ lakh. Two offices have expenses inflated by ₹11 lakh each, as if vehicle hire was paid for vehicles never used. The code below creates exactly the same data as before.

Code to create the block office data
set.seed(4012)
n <- 120
district_terrain <- c(Etawah = "Plain", Sitapur = "Plain", Firozpur = "Plain",
                      Almora = "Hilly", Chamba = "Hilly",
                      Bastar = "Tribal", Mayurbhanj = "Tribal")
bdo <- tibble(
  office_id = sprintf("BDO-%03d", 1:n),
  district  = sample(names(district_terrain), n, replace = TRUE,
                     prob = c(0.17, 0.17, 0.16, 0.13, 0.12, 0.13, 0.12))
) |>
  mutate(
    terrain = factor(district_terrain[district], levels = c("Plain", "Hilly", "Tribal")),
    # offices in the hills and tribal areas are smaller
    staff = round(case_when(terrain == "Plain" ~ runif(n, 22, 55),
                            terrain == "Hilly" ~ runif(n, 12, 38),
                            TRUE               ~ runif(n, 16, 44))),
    sanctioned    = round(staff / runif(n, 0.80, 0.95)),
    # bigger offices have bigger buildings
    area_sqm      = round(120 + 11 * staff + rnorm(n, 0, 45)),
    population    = round(runif(n, 60, 200) * if_else(terrain == "Plain", 1.2, 0.8)),
    beneficiaries = round(population * runif(n, 0.25, 0.35), 1),
    # office expenses in lakh, driven by staff, beneficiaries and terrain
    office_exp = round(5 + 0.45 * staff + 0.12 * beneficiaries +
                       c(Plain = 0, Hilly = 6, Tribal = 3.5)[as.character(terrain)] +
                       rnorm(n, 0, 1.6), 2)
  )

# Planted cases. Vehicle hire of Rs 11 lakh paid without any vehicle used
planted <- c("BDO-027", "BDO-088")
bdo <- bdo |>
  mutate(office_exp = if_else(office_id %in% planted, office_exp + 11, office_exp))

We will compare our new models with the model of staff alone, lin_reg1, from Chapter 37. So let us fit it again.

lin_reg1 <- lm(office_exp ~ staff, data = bdo)

38.3 Multiple linear regression

In multiple linear regression, several independent variables together explain the dependent variable. The regression equation becomes

\[ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_1 + \beta_2x_2 + \beta_3x_3 + ... + \epsilon \tag{38.1}\]

where \(x_1\), \(x_2\), \(x_3\) …, \(x_n\) are the \(n\) independent variables and \(y\) is the dependent variable as usual.

With two predictors, this equation is no longer a line but a flat plane in three dimensions. With more predictors, it is a hyperplane, which we cannot draw. So we usually look at the predictors one at a time, or use correlation plots.

As we noted in Chapter 37, there is one more assumption now. The independent variables should not be strongly related to each other, that is, there should be no multi-collinearity. Let us build an example. We just add the predictors to the formula using the + operator.

Example 2. Staff strength alone explained about 60% of the variation in office expenses. Let us try to explain office expenses using staff strength staff, floor area of the building area_sqm and the number of beneficiaries beneficiaries together. First, let us visualise each relationship separately, in Figure 38.1.

Let us also visualise the correlation between all these variables. See Figure 38.2.

Notice that two of the predictors are strongly correlated with each other too. Staff strength and floor area have a correlation of 0.93. Offices with more staff need bigger buildings. Keep this in mind. Now let us build the model.

data2 <- bdo[, c("office_exp", "staff", "area_sqm", "beneficiaries")]
lin_reg2 <- lm(office_exp ~ .,
               data = data2)
summary(lin_reg2)

In the formula, we used . instead of naming all the variables. The . is a shorthand for “all other columns of the data”. There is now one slope for each predictor and one intercept. The equation can be written as Equation 38.2.

\[ \text{Office expenses} = 14.2784 + 0.3431 \cdot \text{Staff} - 0.002411 \cdot \text{Area} + 0.0657 \cdot \text{Beneficiaries} \tag{38.2}\]

Interpretation. The slopes of staff and beneficiaries are positive, so office expenses rise with both. Each slope is read keeping the other variables constant. For example, if two offices have the same floor area and the same number of beneficiaries, the one with 1 more employee spends ₹0.343 lakh more a year. Similarly, 1,000 more beneficiaries add ₹0.066 lakh, for the same staff and floor area. “Keeping other variables constant” is important. If the other variables also change, the effect on expenses is different.

Results. The model explains about 64% of the variation in office expenses (Multiple R-squared), against 60% with staff alone. Adjusted R-squared is a little lower, at 63%, after the penalty for three predictors. The F-test p-value is very small, so at least one slope is not zero.

Look at area_sqm, however. On its own, floor area is clearly related to office expenses (Figure 38.1, middle). In this model, its slope is -0.0024, slightly negative, as if a bigger building cost less to run. Its p-value is 0.70, not significant at all. This is because area_sqm carries almost the same information as staff. Once staff is in the model, area has little new to say, and its slope is left to chance. We could remove area_sqm and re-run the model. We will come back to this when we discuss multi-collinearity.

38.4 Including categorical or factor variables in lm

So far, both our response variable and our predictors were numeric. But what if a predictor is categorical, like the terrain of a block, the city class of an office, or the type of a contract?

In such a case, we should make sure that the categorical variable is of type factor before building the model. We learnt about factors in Chapter 24. The first level of the factor matters, as we will see. We created terrain with the levels in the order Plain, Hilly, Tribal.

Example 3. Let us try to explain office_exp by terrain alone. Offices in the hills and tribal areas need more vehicle hire to cover the same block, so we would expect them to spend more. terrain is already a factor, so we need not convert it. Before moving on, let us visualise the relationship. Box plots are best suited here.

Now let us build the model.

Interpretation. R converts a factor into dummy variables. A dummy variable is 1 when a row belongs to a category and 0 otherwise. Being numeric, the dummies can be used in linear regression as before. The first level of the factor is taken as the baseline. It gets no dummy of its own. terrain has three levels, so it is encoded as two dummy variables. To see this, we can use the contrasts() function.

We can also see the actual numbers that went into the model with model.matrix(). Let us look at the first office of each terrain.

first_rows <- match(levels(bdo$terrain), bdo$terrain)

When the office is in the hills, terrainHilly is 1 and terrainTribal is 0, and the other way round for a tribal area. When both are 0, the office is in the plains, the baseline. That is why there is no separate coefficient for Plain. Now we can write the regression equation as Equation 38.3.

\[ \text{Office expenses} = 27.307 - 1.614 \cdot \text{terrainHilly} - 1.496 \cdot \text{terrainTribal} \tag{38.3}\]

Reading this equation is now easy. For an office in the hills, the predicted expense is 27.307 - 1.614, or 25.693. When both dummy variables are zero, the predicted expense is just the intercept. So the intercept is the prediction for the baseline level, Plain, and each slope is the difference from the baseline. If we remove the intercept by adding - 1 to the formula, R gives us one coefficient for each level directly.

There is a surprise here. Offices in the hills and tribal areas seem to spend less than offices in the plains, not more. And the p-values of the two slopes in lm_fact, 0.10 and 0.15, say that terrain makes no real difference at all. Should we conclude that terrain does not matter? Not yet. We will find the answer in the next section.

WarningCaution

Look at the R-squared of the two models. With an intercept it is 0.030. Without an intercept it jumps to 0.972. The fit has not improved at all, since the predictions are exactly the same. Without an intercept, R measures R-squared against zero instead of against the mean, which makes it look much larger. So use - 1 to read the coefficients easily, but compare models using the version with an intercept.

38.4.1 Parallel slopes regression

To understand how a categorical predictor works together with a numeric one, let us build a model step by step.

Example 4. Let us explain office expenses office_exp by staff strength staff and terrain terrain together. Let us visualise the variables first. From Figure 38.4 it is clear that office expenses rise linearly with staff, in all three terrains. Notice also that the offices in the hills and tribal areas are generally smaller.

ggplot(bdo, aes(staff, office_exp)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm", se = FALSE, formula = "y ~ x") +
  facet_wrap(.~ terrain) +
  labs(x = "Staff in position", y = "Office expenses (₹ lakh)") +
  theme_bw()

Step 1. Let us first use only the numeric variable staff to explain office expenses, and look at the coefficients. This is the same model as Example 1 of Chapter 37.

par_slop1 <- lm(office_exp ~ staff, data = bdo)
coef(par_slop1)
(Intercept)       staff 
 15.5084352   0.3477244

We got one intercept and one slope. We can see the line in Figure 38.5 (left).

Step 2. Now let us explain office expenses by terrain only. We also add - 1 to the formula, which removes the common intercept and gives one intercept for each terrain. This is the model lm_fact0 of Example 3. If a variable is not yet a factor, like a year stored as a number, we can convert it inside the formula with factor(). Now see the coefficients.

par_slop2 <- lm(office_exp ~ factor(terrain) - 1, data = bdo)
coef(par_slop2)
 factor(terrain)Plain  factor(terrain)Hilly factor(terrain)Tribal 
             27.30684              25.69265              25.81034

We got one intercept for each terrain. As Figure 38.5 (right) shows, these are nothing but the mean expenses for each terrain. The three means are close to each other.

Step 3. Now we build the complete model with both variables together, and look at the coefficients.

par_slop <- lm(office_exp ~ staff + terrain - 1, data = bdo)
coef(par_slop)
        staff  terrainPlain  terrainHilly terrainTribal 
    0.4416366    11.1599895    15.3011985    12.0282383

There is one slope for the numeric variable and a separate intercept for each terrain. If we draw these, we get three parallel lines with the same slope. See Figure 38.6.

This is also clear from the equation, Equation 38.5.

\[ \text{Office expenses} = 0.4416 \cdot \text{Staff} + 11.1600 \cdot \text{Plain} + 15.3012 \cdot \text{Hilly} + 12.0282 \cdot \text{Tribal} \tag{38.5}\]

For any office, one of the three dummy variables is 1 and the others are 0. The one with 1 acts as the intercept, but the slope stays the same. In other words, whatever the terrain, office expenses change with staff at the same rate.

Notice something interesting. In Step 2, the three terrains had almost the same mean expenses. But now, for the same staff strength, an office in the hills spends about ₹4.1 lakh a year more than an office in the plains. An office in a tribal area spends about ₹0.9 lakh more. The difference was hidden because offices in the hills are smaller on average (23.5 staff against 36.6 in the plains). This is the power of multiple regression. It separates the effect of one variable from another. It also answers the surprise of Example 3. Terrain does matter, once we compare offices of the same size.

But is it right to force the same slope on all terrains? Perhaps each extra employee in the hills costs more, because each one travels more. How do we allow for that? The answer is interaction, discussed next.

38.4.2 Extending multiple linear regression by including interactions

The parallel slopes model forced a common slope on each category. That is not always the best option.

Example 4, continued. In the same example, we can allow an interaction between the two predictors using the operator : in the formula.

new_model <- lm(office_exp ~ staff + terrain + staff:terrain - 1, data = bdo)
coef(new_model)
              staff        terrainPlain        terrainHilly       terrainTribal 
         0.41006413         12.31432196         13.90042255         11.71979650 
 staff:terrainHilly staff:terrainTribal 
         0.09110541          0.04145620

Notice the two extra coefficients. Each is the change in slope when we move from the baseline terrain, Plain, to Hilly or to Tribal. So the model now has a different slope for each terrain. See Figure 38.7.

We may write the equation now (Equation 38.6).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Office expenses} = {} & 0.4101 \cdot \text{Staff} + 12.3143 \cdot \text{Plain} + 13.9004 \cdot \text{Hilly} + 11.7198 \cdot \text{Tribal} \\ & + 0.0911 \cdot \text{Staff} \cdot \text{Hilly} + 0.0415 \cdot \text{Staff} \cdot \text{Tribal} \end{aligned} \tag{38.6}\]

So for offices in the hills, the slope of staff is \(0.4101 + 0.0911\), or 0.5012. But look at the p-values of the two interaction terms, 0.18 and 0.54. They are far above 0.05. So the data does not give us any real evidence that the slopes differ between the terrains. The three lines in Figure 38.7 are nearly parallel too. Here the simpler parallel slopes model is good enough. Always check whether an interaction is significant before reading meaning into it. We will see an interaction which does matter in Section 38.9.

We can add as many interactions as we like using :. When there are many, we can use another shorthand, *. So x*z means x + z + x:z, and x*z*w means x + z + w + x:z + x:w + z:w + x:z:w. It does not change the model, but saves a lot of typing.

38.5 Multi-collinearity and the variance inflation factor (VIF)

Multi-collinearity means that the predictor variables are strongly related to each other. This creates a problem. When two predictors move together, the model cannot tell which of them is really affecting \(y\). The coefficient estimates become unstable. A small change in the data can change them a lot, sometimes even flip their sign. Their standard errors become large, so useful variables may look insignificant. We saw this with area_sqm in Example 2.

Note that multi-collinearity does not spoil the predictions of the model much. It spoils our ability to interpret the individual coefficients. For an auditor, who usually wants to explain a finding, that matters.

There are many ways to detect multi-collinearity. One is to use correlation plots, as in Figure 38.2. Another is the Variance Inflation Factor or VIF. To find the VIF of a predictor, we regress it on all the other predictors and note the \(R^2\) of that regression. The VIF score thus tells us how well a predictor is explained by the other predictors.

\[ {VIF} = \frac{1}{1-R^2} \tag{38.7}\]

So the closer this \(R^2\) is to \(1\), the higher the \({VIF}\).

  • \(VIF\) starts at \(1\) and has no upper limit.
  • \({VIF} = 1\) means the predictor is not related to the other predictors at all.
  • \({VIF}\) above \(5\) is commonly taken to indicate a problem, and above \(10\) a serious one.

In R, we do not have to calculate \({VIF}\) by hand. The function vif() from the package car does it. Let us first check our model of Example 2.

car::vif(lin_reg2)
        staff      area_sqm beneficiaries 
     7.507741      7.599050      1.109000

The VIFs of staff and area_sqm are 7.5 and 7.6, both above 5. That confirms what we suspected. The VIF of beneficiaries is close to 1, so it brings its own information. Now let us try a model where office expenses are explained by all the numeric columns of our data. Sanctioned posts move with staff in position, and beneficiaries move with the population of the block. We again use the shorthand . for all other variables.

bdo_num <- bdo |>
  select(office_exp, staff, sanctioned, area_sqm, population, beneficiaries)
mod <- lm(office_exp ~ . , data = bdo_num)
car::vif(mod)
        staff    sanctioned      area_sqm    population beneficiaries 
    46.598673     39.612223      7.630437     16.333157     16.109711

Four predictors here have a VIF above 10. Such a model would be hard to interpret. In practice, we would keep only one predictor from each related pair, the one which makes more business sense. For office expenses, staff in position matters more than sanctioned posts, because vacant posts spend nothing.

TipAudit tip

Many indicators in government data come in related pairs. Sanctioned posts and staff in position, population and beneficiaries, budget and expenditure, length and cost of a road. Putting both members of a pair in one model rarely helps. Pick the one closer to the actual cause of the expenditure. Chapter 43 shows another way, which combines related indicators into one.

38.6 Audit use case, block offices with inflated expenses

Let us now return to the two block offices we planted. We have learnt a good deal about their data. Office expenses rise with staff and with beneficiaries. Offices in the hills and tribal areas spend more for the same size. Floor area, sanctioned posts and population add little once staff and beneficiaries are in the model. So the model which makes business sense uses staff, beneficiaries and terrain.

bdo_final <- lm(office_exp ~ staff + beneficiaries + terrain, data = bdo)

38.8 Simpson’s paradox

Edward Hugh Simpson, a statistician and former cryptanalyst at Bletchley Park, described this phenomenon in a paper in 1951. It is a classic example of how a regression that leaves out an important variable can mislead. The paradox arises when a trend seen in each subgroup of the data reverses or disappears when the subgroups are combined. We saw a mild form of it in Example 4, where terrain seemed not to matter until we compared offices of the same size.

Example 6. A health department runs 60 hospitals of three types. There are primary health centres (PHCs), community health centres (CHCs) and district hospitals. For each we know the number of patients treated in the year (patients, in thousand) and the cost per patient (cost_pp, in ₹). Does a hospital that treats more patients spend less per patient? Let us try to establish a relationship between the two. See the plots in Figure 38.12.

Code to create the hospital data
set.seed(77)
hosp_types <- c("PHC", "CHC", "District hospital")
hosp <- tibble(type = factor(rep(hosp_types, each = 20), levels = hosp_types)) |>
  mutate(
    patients = round(case_when(type == "PHC" ~ runif(n(), 5, 15),
                               type == "CHC" ~ runif(n(), 18, 35),
                               TRUE          ~ runif(n(), 40, 80)), 1),
    # within each type, cost per patient falls as the number of patients grows
    base    = c(PHC = 950, CHC = 1700, "District hospital" = 3300)[as.character(type)],
    centre  = c(PHC = 10, CHC = 26, "District hospital" = 60)[as.character(type)],
    cost_pp = round(base - 18 * (patients - centre) + rnorm(n(), 0, 80))
  ) |>
  select(type, patients, cost_pp)

In the left plot, the relationship is positive. Hospitals treating more patients seem to spend more per patient. But when the hidden variable type is exposed (right), we see a negative relationship within each type. A busier PHC costs less per patient than a quieter PHC, and the same holds for CHCs and district hospitals. Let us confirm this with two models.

Without type, the slope of patients is 41.53. Once type is in the model, it becomes -19.11. The sign has flipped. Type of hospital is a confounding variable here. District hospitals treat more patients and treat costlier cases. An auditor who compared all hospitals together might wrongly conclude that busy hospitals are wasteful. So before finalising a model, we must make sure that we are not missing an important variable. To avoid being misled by Simpson’s paradox, we should

  • Identify confounding variables. Think about what else could affect both variables under study.
  • Consider all levels. Analyse the data at the subgroup level and at the combined level, and compare.
  • Use statistical techniques such as multiple regression, as we just did, to control for confounding variables.
  • Report transparently. Clearly state the method, assumptions and limitations of the analysis so that others can evaluate the findings.
TipAudit tip

Simpson’s paradox is common in audit data. A scheme may show a falling rejection rate in every district, yet a rising rate for the state as a whole, because the mix of districts has changed. Before reporting a trend on combined data, always check it within the main subgroups, like districts, years, or types of contract.

38.9 Audit use case, offices that spend more than expected

We have used regression on audit data throughout this chapter. Let us now put it all together on the question with which we started. Salary expenditure of an office should depend mainly on its staff strength. If an office spends far more than its staff strength explains, it may have ghost employees on its pay roll, or other irregular payments. Regression gives us an expected expenditure for every office. The residual is then the unexplained part. Large positive residuals are our leads.

38.9.1 Our sample data

We create the data of 90 sub-divisional offices (SDOs) of an imaginary department. For each office we have its staff strength, the class of the city where it is located, and its monthly salary expenditure in ₹ lakh. House rent allowance (HRA) is 30%, 20% and 10% of pay in X, Y and Z class cities respectively. So, for the same staff, an office in an X class city spends more.

We have planted a few cases.

  • Three offices have salary expenditure inflated by 16%, as if ghost employees were added.
  • In one office, the staff strength was keyed in with an extra zero.

As before, new readers may skip the code which creates the data.

38.9.5 What the auditor does next

The model has given us 2 leads and one data quality finding. None of them is yet an audit finding. For each lead, we would

  • obtain the pay roll of the office for a few months, and count the employees actually paid against the staff strength reported,
  • match the employees paid with the attendance register, the service books and the bank accounts into which pay was credited, looking for the same account shared by two employees, or employees who never mark attendance,
  • ask the office to explain the excess of about ₹10.9 lakh a month, in writing, and
  • record the model, the residuals and the replies in the working papers, so that the selection of these offices can be justified later.

The keying error in the staff strength of SDO-81 should be reported as a weakness in the data, because the same figure may be used elsewhere, for example to sanction posts or to allot funds.

38.10 Uses of regression in audit

We have seen that regression analysis is a powerful tool for an auditor. It may be used, among other things, for the following.

  • Detection of anomalies and outliers. As in our office examples, regression gives the expected value of an amount based on its drivers. Large residuals point to transactions or units which do not fit, and these may be errors, irregularities or fraud.
  • Analytical procedures. The auditor can estimate what an expenditure should be, say fuel cost from vehicle kilometres, or electricity charges from the connected load, and compare it with what was booked.
  • Risk assessment. Units with large unexplained deviations can be ranked and taken up first in the audit plan. This helps the auditor use limited time and staff well.
  • Benchmarking. Regression can compare the performance of an organisation with similar ones, after adjusting for size and other factors. Deviations from the expected relationship highlight areas that deserve a closer look. The cost of road works per kilometre, adjusted for width, terrain and lead, is one example.

38.11 A suggested workflow

For any regression analysis in audit, we may follow these steps.

  1. Understand the drivers. Decide, from knowledge of the business, which variables should explain the amount under audit. Regression only confirms or rejects relationships which make sense.
  2. Visualise first. Draw scatter plots and box plots to see whether the relationships look linear, and to spot obvious errors.
  3. Fit a simple model with lm(), and read its summary().
  4. Check influence. Look at Cook’s distance and leverage. Investigate and correct data entry errors before going further.
  5. Check the assumptions with plot() or check_model(). Check multi-collinearity with vif() when there are several predictors, and keep only one predictor from each related pair.
  6. Improve the model. Add factors and interactions that make business sense. Watch for hidden variables which can reverse a trend. Compare models with adjusted R-squared or anova().
  7. Flag the residuals. Use augment() and pick the units with large standardised residuals. Quantify the excess in rupees.
  8. Verify with records. Examine the documents of the flagged units before drawing any conclusion.
ImportantRemember

A regression model shows association, not cause. A large residual means only that a unit does not fit the pattern of the others. It is a lead, not a finding. There may be a genuine reason which the model does not know about. Always examine the records before reporting.
