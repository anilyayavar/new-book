64  Payables and Vendors

Last updated

23 October 2026

A State Directorate of Health Services buys paper, toner, bulbs, cement, chairs and medicines for its eight district offices. Each office raises orders, receives the goods, accepts the bills and pays the vendors. Thousands of payments go out in a year. Most are routine. A few are not. A vendor’s bank account is changed for twelve days, just long enough for one large payment. A firm is registered on a Monday and paid on the following Monday. One office pays almost 40% more than its neighbours for the same toner cartridge, month after month. None of this shows in a payment register read line by line.

Payables are where an organisation’s money leaves it. So they attract both error and fraud. Earlier chapters of this book already hold many tests that apply here. They find duplicate invoices, bills split below a limit, look-alike vendor names and links between employees and vendors. This chapter brings those tests together for one department’s payables, in a few lines each. It then adds the classic payables tests that the book has not yet covered. These are a review of the vendor master and its change log, and the three-way match of order, receipt and invoice. They also include price benchmarking of the same item across offices and months. Finally it combines the flags into a view of each vendor, and follows two cases to the end.

As in every chapter of this part, a flag is not a finding. Most flagged items will have a good reason. The aim is to find the few that need a question.

64.1 The purchase-to-pay cycle

Every purchase passes through the same steps. Auditors call them the purchase-to-pay cycle. Each step leaves a record, and each record can be checked against the others.

The flow is simple. Vendor, then order, then receipt, then invoice, then payment. The controls are simple too. Nobody should be paid unless the vendor is genuine, the goods were ordered, the goods arrived, and the bill matches both. The tests in this chapter check each of these, for every transaction.

64.2 Our data, the payables of a State health directorate

Our auditee is an imaginary Directorate of Health Services of a State, with eight district offices. We have its purchase and payment records for 2024-25. All names, firms and numbers are made up. There are nine tables.

  • vendors is the vendor master. Each vendor has an id, a name, a PAN, a GSTIN, a bank account, an address and the date it was registered.
  • vendor_changes is the change log of the vendor master. It records each change of bank account, with the old and new values, the date and the user who made it.
  • employees is the employee master, with each employee’s office, designation, PAN, bank account and address.
  • purchase_orders has one row for each order. To keep the example simple, each order is for one item, with a quantity and a rate. With several lines in an order, the same joins work on the order number and the line number.
  • receipts has one row for each delivery. challan_date is the date on the vendor’s delivery challan, when the goods arrived. grn_date is the date the store entered the goods receipt note in the system. Some orders arrive in two deliveries.
  • invoices has one row for each bill, with the order it refers to, the quantity, the rate and the amount. Rates include tax.
  • payments has one row for each payment, with the bank account it was paid into. Bills are paid in full, without deductions, to keep the example simple.
  • past_paid holds the total paid to each vendor in each of the three earlier years, from the old accounting system.
  • rate_contract holds the State rate contract for cement, and bank_totals the monthly total of payments in the department’s bank statement.

Most of the data is routine. Offices buy from two or three regular vendors for each item. Prices differ a little by office, since remote offices pay more for transport. Large orders get a better rate, and prices rise slowly through the year. Some vendors changed their bank accounts for good reasons. Some goods receipts were entered in the system a few days late. Into this, these cases have been planted.

  1. A bank account changed around a large payment. Narmada Office Solutions, a regular supplier of paper, has its account changed three days before a large payment, and changed back nine days after it.
  2. A vendor paid within a week of registration. Trinetra Medical Agencies is registered, given an order, and paid within seven days. It has no GSTIN, and it is paid large sums over the following months.
  3. A dormant vendor wakes up. Gomti Furniture House was last paid three years ago. In February 2025 it receives large orders of chairs.
  4. A duplicate vendor. Maa Durga Medical Agency is registered with the same PAN as Maa Durga Medical Agencies, a slightly different name, and a different bank account.
  5. A vendor sharing a bank account with an employee. Sagar Lighting House is paid into the account of an employee of the Madhopur office.
  6. A GSTIN that fails its check. One character of the GSTIN of Pragati Paper Mart is wrong.
  7. Breaks in the three-way match. Invoices with no order, invoices dated before the order, more billed than received, rates billed above the order, payments made before the goods arrived, and orders received in part but paid in full.
  8. One office paying more. The Dhanpur office buys toner cartridges from one vendor at about 40% above what other offices pay.

There are a few more, which the tests of earlier chapters will find. Let us build the data.

64.4.6 PAN and GSTIN

A PAN has a fixed format, and a GSTIN has a check character. Section 21.10 and Section 21.11 explained both. We reuse the GSTIN check in a compact form. A valid GSTIN must also contain the vendor’s own PAN, in characters 3 to 12.

gst_chars <- c(0:9, LETTERS)
gstin_valid <- function(x) {
  map_lgl(x, function(g) {
    if (is.na(g) || !str_detect(g, "^[0-9]{2}[A-Z]{5}[0-9]{4}[A-Z][1-9A-Z]Z[0-9A-Z]$")) return(FALSE)
    v <- match(strsplit(str_sub(g, 1, 14), "")[[1]], gst_chars) - 1
    p <- v * rep(c(1, 2), 7)
    total <- sum(p %/% 36 + p %% 36)
    gst_chars[((36 - total %% 36) %% 36) + 1] == str_sub(g, 15, 15)
  })
}

64.5 The three-way match

The oldest control in payables is the three-way match. Before a bill is paid, three documents must agree. The purchase order says what was ordered and at what rate. The goods receipt says what actually arrived. The invoice says what the vendor is charging. The office should pay for the quantity received, at the rate ordered, and nothing more. Any difference must be explained before payment.

WarningCaution, comparing like with like

A higher price is not always a worse price. Quality differs, as with an original toner cartridge against a compatible one. Small orders cost more per unit. Remote offices pay more for transport. Prices rise during the year, and in a shortage. So compare only the same item, of the same specification, in the same period. Use the median, not the lowest price, as the benchmark. Choose a margin that absorbs the normal spread, here 25%, and write it down before looking at the results. Then ask the office for its reasons before calling any difference excess. The excess is an estimate of what the department could have saved, and it is only as good as the comparison behind it.

64.9.1 What the auditor does next

  1. Ask the department for the request behind each change. A genuine change comes with a letter from the vendor and a cancelled cheque or bank certificate. Check who verified it, and how.
  2. Confirm with the vendor, directly and not through the office, whether it received ₹13.6 lakh in January 2025, and into which account.
  3. Ask the bank, through the department, for the name of the holder of the temporary account.
  4. Find out why DEO04 had the right to change bank details, and whether any second person approved the change. A system that lets one user change an account without approval is a control weakness in itself, whatever the outcome of this case.
  5. Widen the test. Ask for the change log of earlier years, and for changes of other fields, such as the address or mobile number to which payment advices are sent.

  1. Ask the Dhanpur office how the vendor was chosen and how the rate was fixed. Was there a quotation, a tender or a rate contract? Were other vendors invited? Link to Chapter 61 if there was a tender.
  2. Compare the specification. If Dhanpur buys original cartridges and others buy compatible ones, the difference may be fair, though the choice itself may then need a reason. Check the printers in use.
  3. Inspect the stock and the issue register, to see that the cartridges bought were received and used.
  4. Check the vendor’s other links. Is it linked to any employee of the office, through the tests of Section 64.4.5 or a network as in Section 55.5?
  5. Report the excess as an estimate, with the method, the benchmark, the margin and the cautions, and invite the department’s reply.

64.11 A suggested workflow

  1. Get the full purchase-to-pay data, including the vendor change log, employee master and earlier years’ payments. Record who extracted it and when.
  2. Check completeness. Unique keys, every payment linked to an invoice and a vendor, and monthly totals agreeing with the bank.
  3. Review the vendor master. New vendors paid fast, dormant vendors woken up, bank changes near payments and reversed changes, duplicate PANs and bank accounts, links with employees, PAN and GSTIN checks, and large payments to vendors with no GSTIN.
  4. Run the three-way match on every invoice. No order, invoice before order, more billed than received, rate above the order, payment before receipt, and paid more than the value received.
  5. Separate recording delays from real breaks, using challan dates or other evidence, before treating any break as an exception.
  6. Benchmark prices against the median of the same item and period, with a margin fixed in advance. Estimate the excess, and state it as an estimate.
  7. Add the tests of earlier chapters. Duplicates, split bills, look-alike names, shared addresses and the rate in force.
  8. Combine the flags by vendor, and rank. Use weights only with written reasons.
  9. Follow up the top vendors with documents, inspection and confirmation, and record what cleared each flag.
ImportantRemember
  • The vendor master decides who can be paid. Review it, and its change log, before the transactions.
  • A bank account changed and changed back around one payment is one of the clearest signs of payment diversion. Only the change log shows it.
  • The three-way match asks three questions. Was it ordered, did it arrive, and does the bill agree? Payment should follow all three.
  • Many breaks are recording delays. Use a second date or a document to tell them apart.
  • Benchmark prices against the median of like purchases, and report the excess as an estimate, with its cautions.
  • One flag is often innocent. Several flags on one vendor deserve the auditor’s time.
