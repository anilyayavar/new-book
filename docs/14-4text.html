48  Text Analytics

Text analytics is crucial in data analytics, as text data is becoming increasingly important in almost every field. A large part of the information in any organisation is in the form of text, not numbers. To make full use of it, we need to know how to clean, process, summarise and analyse text. In this chapter, we will learn the basic tools for working with text in R. In the next chapter, we will use them for sentiment analysis, and in the one after that, for visualising text.

Text brings its own challenges. Unlike a table of numbers, text has no fixed structure. The same thing can be written in many ways, with different spellings, abbreviations, cases and punctuation. People mix languages, as in “camp ke baad hospital le gaye”. Words can mean different things in different contexts. So, before analysing text, we must first clean it and bring it into a form that a computer can count.

48.1 Where does an auditor meet text?

Auditors deal with text far more often than they may realise.

  • Public grievances. Complaints received through portals like CPGRAMS, or through scheme helplines. They often describe problems long before these show up in accounts.
  • Narrations in accounts. Every voucher and journal entry has a description, like “being payment for…” or “adjustment as per instructions”. Unusual narrations are a standard red flag in fraud risk assessment.
  • Audit reports and inspection reports. Past observations, which can be searched for recurring issues.
  • Contract and tender documents. Specifications, terms and conditions, and correspondence.
  • Feedback and survey responses. Comments from beneficiaries, patients or citizens.
  • Free-text fields in databases. Remarks columns, reasons for rejection, descriptions of goods.

In each case, reading every document is impossible when there are thousands of them. Text analytics helps the auditor see what is being said, how often, and where.

48.2 Prerequisites

We will mainly use the package tidytext, which brings text analysis into the familiar tidyverse way of working. We will also use SnowballC for stemming.

library(tidyverse)
library(tidytext)
library(SnowballC)

48.4 Cleaning text

48.4.1 Masking personal information

Before anything else, remember that grievances contain personal information, like phone numbers. Before analysing, sharing or quoting such text, mask it. A mobile number in India has ten digits and starts with 6, 7, 8 or 9. The regular expression \\b[6-9][0-9]{9}\\b matches exactly such a number. We learnt regular expressions in Chapter 19.

grievances <- grievances |>
  mutate(text = str_replace_all(text, "\\b[6-9][0-9]{9}\\b", "[PHONE]"))

grievances |>
  filter(str_detect(text, "PHONE")) |>
  slice_head(n = 3) |>
  pull(text)
[1] "Respected sir, Hospital staff demanded Rs 3000 from us for medicines although treatment is free under the scheme . Mobile no [PHONE] ."      
[2] "Sir ji, Hospital staff demanded Rs 1500 from us for medicines although treatment is free under the scheme. Mobile no [PHONE]."               
[3] "Respected sir, Agent of the hospital told villagers that free operation is available, took us in their van on Sunday. Contact me on [PHONE]."
WarningCaution

Audit teams must handle personal data with great care. Mask phone numbers, Aadhaar numbers, bank account numbers and names before the data leaves the secure environment. Never put such data into online tools, including AI chatbots, unless your organisation explicitly permits it.

48.4.2 Basic cleaning

Next, we make the text uniform. We convert it to lower case, remove the masked phone tags and other numbers, and squeeze extra spaces. All these functions come from stringr, which we learnt in Chapter 18.

grievances <- grievances |>
  mutate(clean_text = text |>
           str_to_lower() |>
           str_remove_all("\\[phone\\]") |>
           str_remove_all("[0-9]+") |>
           str_squish())

grievances |>
  select(text, clean_text) |>
  slice(17)
# A tibble: 1 × 2
  text                                                                clean_text
  <chr>                                                               <chr>     
1 Agent of the hospital told villagers that free opration is availab… agent of …

We have kept the original text column untouched, and created a new clean_text column. This is a good habit. We can always go back to the original to read the full complaint.

Should we remove numbers? It depends on the question. Here we only want words. But if we wanted to know how much money was demanded, the numbers would be the most important part. We could extract them first with str_extract().

48.5 Tokenisation

Computers cannot understand sentences, but they can count. So we break the text into small units, usually single words, called tokens. This step is called tokenisation.

unnest_tokens() from tidytext does this. It takes a column of text, and produces a new data frame with one row per token. All other columns, like the grievance ID and category, are repeated for each word. This is called the tidy text format.

tokens <- grievances |>
  select(grievance_id, hospital_id, category, clean_text) |>
  unnest_tokens(output = word, input = clean_text)

TipBuilding a list of domain stop words

There is no ready list of domain stop words. Look at the top words, remove the ones that carry no meaning for your question, and look again. Two or three rounds are usually enough. But be careful not to remove a word only because it is common. In our data, operation is common, and it is very important.

48.7 Stemming

The same word appears in different forms. Operation, operations and operated. Demand, demanded and demanding. For counting, we may want to treat these as one. Stemming cuts words down to their root, called the stem. wordStem() from the package SnowballC does this.

wordStem(c("operation", "operations", "operated", "demand", "demanded",
           "removed", "removal", "discharged", "discharge"))
[1] "oper"     "oper"     "oper"     "demand"   "demand"   "remov"    "remov"   
[8] "discharg" "discharg"

The stems are not always proper words. Operation becomes oper. But all forms of a word now look the same, and will be counted together.

tokens_clean |>
  mutate(stem = wordStem(word)) |>
  count(stem, sort = TRUE) |>
  head(10)
# A tibble: 10 × 2
   stem          n
   <chr>     <int>
 1 dai         229
 2 card        202
 3 oper        179
 4 treatment   132
 5 staff       118
 6 father      114
 7 pend        106
 8 discharg    102
 9 doctor       87
10 ward         82
NoteStemming or not?

Stemming helps when the exact form of a word does not matter, for example when counting themes. It is not helpful when we want to read and present the words, because stems like oper look odd in a report. A more refined method, called lemmatisation, reduces words to proper dictionary forms. For most audit work, simple stemming or no stemming at all is enough.

48.8 Pairs of words, n-grams

Single words lose their context. Removed alone tells little, but uterus removed tells a lot. A sequence of n words is called an n-gram. Two words together are a bigram. unnest_tokens() can produce these too, with token = "ngrams" and n = 2.

bigrams <- grievances |>
  select(grievance_id, hospital_id, category, clean_text) |>
  unnest_tokens(bigram, clean_text, token = "ngrams", n = 2)

Each bigram overlaps with the next one. To remove stop words from bigrams, we separate the two words into two columns, and keep only bigrams in which neither word is a stop word.

all_stops <- c(stop_words$word, my_stop_words$word)

bigrams_clean <- bigrams |>
  separate_wider_delim(bigram, delim = " ", names = c("word1", "word2")) |>
  filter(!word1 %in% all_stops, !word2 %in% all_stops) |>
  unite(bigram, word1, word2, sep = " ")

Such problems do not fit neatly into any category.

48.9 Finding themes with a keyword dictionary

So far we have looked at what words are common. For audit, we usually have a sharper question. How many complaints are about money being demanded? About camps? About unnecessary surgery? A simple and very effective way to answer this is a keyword dictionary. We list a few words or patterns for each theme, and tag every grievance that matches.

themes <- tribble(
  ~theme,              ~pattern,
  "Money demanded",    "demand|paid|pay |took money|took rs|cash|extra|receipt",
  "Camp or agent",     "camp|vehicle|van |agent|village",
  "Surgery concerns",  "uterus|without any test|second operation|wound|in a hurry|operation kar",
  "Denial or card",    "refused|not valid|not accepted|sent us away|server",
  "Delay",             "pending|delayed|not discharged|wait",
  "Poor facilities",   "dirty|rude|toilet|food|drinking water|visited only"
)

themes
# A tibble: 6 × 2
  theme            pattern                                                      
  <chr>            <chr>                                                        
1 Money demanded   demand|paid|pay |took money|took rs|cash|extra|receipt       
2 Camp or agent    camp|vehicle|van |agent|village                              
3 Surgery concerns uterus|without any test|second operation|wound|in a hurry|op…
4 Denial or card   refused|not valid|not accepted|sent us away|server

The patterns are regular expressions, so | means “or”. Now we check each grievance against each pattern. str_detect() returns TRUE where the pattern is found. A grievance can belong to more than one theme.

tagged <- grievances |>
  select(grievance_id, hospital_id, category, clean_text) |>
  cross_join(themes) |>
  filter(str_detect(clean_text, pattern)) |>
  select(-pattern)

tagged |>
  count(theme, sort = TRUE)
# A tibble: 6 × 2
  theme                n
  <chr>            <int>
1 Denial or card     178
2 Poor facilities    159
3 Delay              152
4 Money demanded     149
5 Surgery concerns    99
6 Camp or agent       80

cross_join() pairs every grievance with every theme. filter() then keeps only the pairs where the grievance text matches the theme’s pattern.

TipAudit tip

A keyword dictionary is simple, transparent and easy to explain in an audit report. Build it by reading a sample of the text, test it on another sample, and refine it. Keep the patterns specific. A pattern like pay alone would also match “payment pending” and “salary paid”. That is why we used pay with a space, and took money.

48.10 Words that set a group apart, tf-idf

Counting words shows what is common. But often we want to know what is distinctive about one group compared with others. For this, text analysts use a measure called tf-idf, short for term frequency, inverse document frequency.

  • Term frequency (tf) is how often a word appears in a group, as a share of all words in that group.
  • Inverse document frequency (idf) is high for words that appear in only a few groups, and zero for words that appear in all groups.
  • tf-idf multiplies the two. So it is high for words that are frequent in one group and rare in others.

bind_tf_idf() from tidytext calculates it. We need the word counts by group. Here, a “group” is a grievance category.

category_tfidf <- tokens_clean |>
  count(category, word) |>
  bind_tf_idf(word, category, n)

category_tfidf |>
  slice_max(tf_idf, n = 5, by = category, with_ties = FALSE) |>
  select(category, word, tf_idf) |>
  print(n = 30)

48.11 Audit use case, narrations of journal entries

The same tools can be used on the narrations of accounting entries. Standards on auditing ask auditors to test journal entries for signs of fraud, especially entries made by senior staff, at the end of the year, for round amounts, or with vague descriptions. Let us create the journal entries of an imaginary organisation for 2024-25. Most of them are routine. We have planted 20 suspicious entries, with vague narrations, round amounts and mostly posted by the finance manager, many in the last days of March.

Code to create the journal entries
set.seed(2402)
n <- 3000
templates <- c("Being payment of electricity bill for MONTH", "Being salary for the month of MONTH",
               "Being purchase of stationery vide bill no NUM", "Being payment to contractor, RA bill NUM",
               "Being advance to staff for tour, adjusted against TA bill NUM", "Being depreciation for MONTH",
               "Being interest accrued on deposits for MONTH", "Being transfer of grant received to project account",
               "Being rent for office building for MONTH", "Being provision for audit fee",
               "Being reversal of duplicate entry no NUM, approved vide note no NUM")

journal <- tibble(
  entry_no  = sprintf("JE%05d", 1:n),

48.12 Audit use case, brand names in tender specifications

Specifications in a tender should describe what is needed, not whose product. The General Financial Rules require specifications to be generic, and where a brand has to be mentioned as a reference, it should be followed by the words “or equivalent”. Specifications written around one brand, or with phrases like “only” and “exclusively”, keep competitors out and may favour a particular supplier.

Let us create 240 item specifications from the tenders of four sections of an imaginary department. The brand names are made up. In the Electrical section, we have planted specifications which name the brand Voltix without “or equivalent”.

Code to create the specifications

48.13 A suggested workflow

  1. Understand the text and the question. Read a sample of the documents before writing any code.
  2. Protect personal data. Mask phone numbers, IDs and names.
  3. Clean the text. Lower case, remove numbers or punctuation if not needed, squeeze spaces. Keep the original column.
  4. Tokenise, into words and, where useful, into bigrams.
  5. Remove stop words, both the standard list and your own domain list.
  6. Count and compare. Look at top words and bigrams, and use tf-idf to see what sets groups apart.
  7. Build a keyword dictionary for the themes that matter to the audit, and tag every document.
  8. Link back to structured data. Count themes by unit, period or category, and compare with other evidence.
  9. Read the actual documents behind every finding before reporting.
ImportantRemember

Text analytics counts words. It does not understand them. A complaint saying “they did not demand any money” contains the word demand. Always read a sample of the documents behind every count, before treating the count as evidence.
