46  Time Series Analysis

Every office keeps figures month by month. A treasury records the expenditure of each department every month. A sub-registrar office records the stamp duty it collects. A commercial tax circle records its tax collections every quarter. An auditor who looks at only one month, or only one year, sees a single number. An auditor who looks at the whole run of months sees a pattern. And a pattern tells us what to expect.

That is the idea behind this chapter. If receipts have grown by about 8% every year, and always peak in March, then we know roughly what this year’s receipts should be. If the actual receipts fall well short, we have a lead. Perhaps some revenue has leaked. In the same way, if a department always spends much more in March, we can measure that March rush, and spot a March which is unusual even for March.

A sequence of values recorded over time, in order, is called a time series. Monthly expenditure, daily cash balance, quarterly GST collections and yearly excise revenue are all time series. Time series analysis means studying such data to understand its patterns, and to predict its future values. So it serves for description as well as prediction.

A few uses in audit are these.

  • Revenue analysis. An auditor may examine revenue over time to find irregular patterns. Unexpected falls, unusual growth, or a change in the usual seasonal pattern may point to misstatement, leakage or fraud. We will see a complete example at the end of the chapter, in Section 46.7.
  • Expenditure analysis. Government offices are known to spend much more in March, to use up the budget before the financial year ends. Time series methods let us measure this March rush, and find months which are unusual even after allowing for it. The anomaly detection chapter takes this further, in Section 47.11.
  • Inventory analysis. Auditors examine stock levels to judge whether they are adequate, and to find obsolescence or shrinkage. Time series methods help us understand the usual pattern of stock, and see when it changes.

In this chapter we will learn how to turn monthly figures into a time series in R, and how to plot it. We will separate a series into its trend and its seasonal pattern. We will forecast it with simple and advanced methods, and test which method forecasts best. Finally, we will use a forecast to judge whether a year’s stamp duty receipts are believable.

46.1 Our data

All the series in this chapter are made up. They are realistic, but no real office or state is meant. We use five of them.

  • spend, the monthly expenditure of a state’s rural development department, in ₹ crore, from April 2014 to March 2024. It grows by about 7% a year, and has a strong March rush. We have planted one irregularity in it. In March 2021 the department spent far more than even its usual March rush. This is the kind of year-end spending that an auditor would want to examine, to see whether funds were parked or drawn only to avoid lapse.
  • fines, the yearly penalties collected by a district transport office, in ₹ lakh, from 1985 to 2024. It goes up and down from year to year, around a steady level.
  • royalty, the yearly mineral royalty collected by a district mining office, in ₹ lakh, from 1975 to 2024. It rises and falls with booms and slumps in construction.
  • ctax, the quarterly commercial tax collections of a state, in ₹ crore, from the April to June quarter of 2005 to the January to March quarter of 2024.
  • excise, the yearly state excise revenue of a state, in ₹ crore, from 1995 to 2023.

New readers may skip the code which creates the data.

To analyse any time series, we have to understand its different components.

46.2 Components of a time series

A time series can be broken down into several components.

  • Level. The baseline value or average of the series. If a series were a straight horizontal line, it would have only a level. The transport penalties in Figure 46.1 (b) wander up and down, but always come back to about the same level. They have no lasting rise or fall.
  • Trend. The long-term direction of the series. It shows whether the series is rising, falling or staying about constant over time. For example, a clear rising trend can be seen in the commercial tax collections in Figure 46.1 (d).
  • Seasonality. A pattern which repeats at a fixed and known interval, like every year, every quarter or every week. In Figure 46.1 (d), commercial tax rises and falls in the same way every year, with the January to March quarter on top. In Figure 46.1 (a), expenditure peaks every March.
  • Cycle. Rises and falls which are not of a fixed length. The mineral royalty in Figure 46.1 (c) rises in construction booms and falls in slumps. Each boom and slump lasts 6 to 10 years, and the length varies from one to the next. That is why they form a cycle and not a season.
  • Errors (residuals). The random ups and downs that cannot be explained by the level, trend, seasonality or cycle. The year-to-year changes in the transport penalties in Figure 46.1 (b) are mostly of this kind. The error component tells us how variable the data is, and so how much we can trust a forecast.

46.3 Additive or multiplicative components

The trend, seasonal and error components can combine with each other in two ways, additive or multiplicative. Which one fits depends on the data.

An additive model assumes that the components are added together. The value of the series at any time is the sum of the trend, seasonal and error components at that time.

\[ y_t = T_t + S_t + e_t \tag{46.1}\]

where \(y_t\), \(T_t\), \(S_t\) and \(e_t\) are the values of the series, trend component, seasonal component and residuals, respectively, at time \(t\). In an additive series, the seasonal ups and downs stay about the same size, whatever the level of the series.

A multiplicative model assumes that the components are multiplied together.

\[ y_t = T_t \times S_t \times e_t \tag{46.2}\]

where \(y_t\), \(T_t\), \(S_t\) and \(e_t\) are defined as before. In a multiplicative series, the seasonal ups and downs grow as the series grows. The seasonal component is then a percentage of the trend, like “March is 40% above an average month”. Look at the expenditure in Figure 46.1 (a). The March peaks rise higher every year, because the March rush is a share of a growing budget. So its seasonality is multiplicative.

We can convert a multiplicative series into an additive one by taking its logarithm, because \(\log(T \times S \times e) = \log T + \log S + \log e\). See Figure 46.2. On the log scale, the March peaks of spend stand about the same height above the other months every year.

We will learn how to separate these components in later sections.

Damped trends. Gardner and McKenzie1 observed in 1985 that most forecasting methods assume a trend will continue forever, however far ahead we forecast. Based on their findings, they suggested that forecasts become more accurate if trends are damped, that is, made to flatten out gradually. We will learn about this idea as well in later sections.

46.4.1 Creating a time series object in R

R provides a simple function ts() to convert a series of observations (basically a vector) into a time series object. The main arguments are

ts(data,
  start = 1,
  end = numeric(),
  frequency = 1,
  ...)

where

  • data is a numeric vector or matrix containing the observations.
  • start is the time of the first observation. It is a single number, like 1985 for yearly data, or a pair of numbers, like c(2022, 4) for April 2022 in monthly data.
  • end is the time of the last observation, written the same way. We usually give only start, and R works out end from the length of the data.
  • frequency is the number of observations per unit of time. Monthly data has a frequency of 12, quarterly data 4, and yearly data 1.
  • ... are other, rarely needed arguments, like names for the columns of a matrix of series.

Let us create a small time series. A block office spent the following amounts, in ₹ lakh, in each month of the financial year 2022-23.

46.5 Time series modelling and forecasting

Forecasting the future is an important part of time series analysis. For an auditor, a forecast is an expectation. It says what the figures should have been if the office behaved as it always did. To forecast, we first need to model the data, that is, describe its pattern. Forecasting techniques can be broadly divided into two groups. Data-based techniques, like averaging, smoothing and decomposition, work directly on the observed values. Model-based techniques, like ARIMA, assume a particular statistical structure for the data and estimate its parameters. In practice the line between the two is not sharp. We will start with the simplest ones.

46.5.1 Naive method

This method assumes that the future value will be the same as the last observed value. It is the simplest and most basic forecasting method, and hence named naive. The formula is

\[ \text{Naive method: }\hat{Y}_{T+1} = Y_T \tag{46.3}\]

Here, \(\hat{Y}_{T+1}\) is the forecast for the next period and \(Y_T\) is the last observed value, in a series of \(T\) observations. So predicting with the naive method needs no special skill. Yet it is useful. Any better method should at least beat the naive forecast.

Let us forecast the transport penalties for 5 future years. The package forecast has a function naive() which does this, once we give the value of the parameter h, short for forecasting horizon. In Figure 46.5, the forecast values (the blue line) are exactly the same as the last observed value. The shaded bands show the range in which the actual values are likely to fall.

forecast::naive(fines, h = 5) %>%
  autoplot() +
  theme_bw() +
  labs(title = "Transport penalties, forecast by the naive method",
       x = "Year", y = "Rs lakh")

By default, 80% and 95% prediction intervals are shown as bands. We can choose other levels with the parameter level, for example level = 90. If we do not want the bands at all, we can use autoplot(..., PI = FALSE).

For seasonal data, there is a variant called the seasonal naive method, snaive(). It forecasts each month as the value of the same month last year. Auditors use this idea all the time, when they compare this March with last March.

TipAudit tip

For a seasonal series, never compare a month with the month just before it. March expenditure will always look alarming next to February. Compare it with the same month of earlier years, as snaive() does, or with a forecast that allows for the season.

46.5.2 Moving averages

In this technique, the forecast is the average of the most recent \(n\) observations. As new data comes in, the window of \(n\) observations slides forward. A moving average smooths a time series by averaging neighbouring values. It reduces noise and reveals the underlying trend.

The formula for forecasting with a moving average is

\[ \hat{Y}_{t+1} = \frac{1}{n}(Y_t + Y_{t-1} + ... + Y_{t-n+1}) \tag{46.4}\]

Here, \(\hat{Y}_{t+1}\) is the forecast for the next period and \(Y_t\), \(Y_{t-1}\), …, \(Y_{t-n+1}\) are the past \(n\) observations.

A moving average can be centred or trailing.

  • In a centred moving average of order \(k\), each value is replaced by the mean of itself, the \((k-1)/2\) values before it and the \((k-1)/2\) values after it. So \(k\) is naturally an odd number. For an even \(k\), like 12 for monthly data, ma() from forecast takes care of centring automatically. Centred averages are used to see the trend in past data.
  • In a trailing moving average, each value is replaced by the mean of itself and the \(k-1\) values before it. It uses only past data, so it is the one we use for forecasting. The function rollmeanr() from zoo calculates it.

Smoothed versions of the monthly expenditure for different \(k\) can be seen in Figure 46.6. A 7-month average softens the March peaks but does not remove them. A 12-month average removes them almost fully, because each average contains exactly one March. Notice also that the trailing average lags behind the data, since it uses only the past.

Let us now forecast with a moving average. For the transport penalties, we take a trailing moving average of the last 7 years with rollmeanr(). The forecast for every future year is then simply the last value of this moving average. Applying naive() to the moving average gives exactly that, as in the left side of Figure 46.7.

In the right side of Figure 46.7, we have done the same with the monthly expenditure. This series has a strong seasonal pattern, so we have used a 12-month moving average. An average over exactly one full year contains every month once, so the seasonal ups and downs cancel out and only the trend remains. We have also drawn the original series in black, so we can see both the original and the smoothed series.

WarningCaution

A moving average forecast is flat. It cannot follow a trend or a seasonal pattern into the future. Look at the expenditure forecast in Figure 46.7. It gives the average month, not the March rush that we can clearly expect. An auditor who used it would find every March “unusual”. For such data we need the methods discussed next.

46.5.3 Exponential smoothing

Exponential smoothing methods forecast with weighted averages of past observations, giving more weight to recent observations. Examples are simple exponential smoothing, Holt’s method and the Holt-Winters method.

Simple exponential smoothing (SES) is the simplest of these. It is a weighted moving average in which the weights fall exponentially as the observations get older. This method is suitable for data with no clear trend or seasonal pattern.

\[ \hat{y}_{T+1} = \alpha{y_T} + \alpha(1- \alpha){y_{T-1}} + \alpha(1 - \alpha)^2{y_{T-2}} + ... \tag{46.5}\]

The smoothing parameter \(\alpha\) lies between \(0\) and \(1\). A value close to 1 gives almost all the weight to the latest observation, so the forecast follows the data closely. A value close to 0 spreads the weight over many past observations, so the forecast is very smooth. An example with \(\alpha = 0.1\), on the transport penalties, is shown in Figure 46.8 (left).

Holt extended SES by adding a trend component, with its own smoothing parameter \(\beta\). The method is named after him as Holt’s linear method. Mathematically, there are now three equations.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y}_{t+h|t} &= l_t + hb_t \\ l_t &= \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)(l_{t-1} + b_{t-1}) \\ b_t &= \beta(l_t - l_{t-1}) + (1-\beta)b_{t-1} \end{aligned} \tag{46.6}\]

Here \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) (again \(0 \le \beta \le 1\)) are the smoothing parameters for level and trend respectively, and \(h\) is the forecast horizon. Also, \(y_t\) is the actual value, \(l_t\) is the level (or intercept) and \(b_t\) is the trend (or slope) of the series at time \(t\). The first equation says that the forecast \(h\) periods ahead is the latest level plus \(h\) times the latest slope. We apply it to the yearly excise revenue, which has a clear upward trend. See Figure 46.8 (right).

To apply SES in R, we use the function ses(), and for Holt’s method, holt(), both from forecast. If we do not give the values of \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), R chooses the best values itself. Here we have fixed them, to see their effect.

As discussed earlier, damped trends often work better. Based on the work of Gardner and McKenzie, a damping parameter \(\phi\) was added. The equations become

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y}_{t+h|t} &= l_t + (\phi + \phi^2 + ... + \phi^h)b_t \\ l_t &= \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)(l_{t-1} + \phi b_{t-1}) \\ b_t &= \beta(l_t - l_{t-1}) + (1-\beta)\phi b_{t-1} \end{aligned} \tag{46.7}\]

If \(\phi = 1\), these are the same as Holt’s linear method. A value \(0 < \phi < 1\) damps the trend, so the forecast line gradually flattens, as shown in Figure 46.9 (left). The two methods are compared in Figure 46.9 (right).

Which one should an auditor prefer? If we expect revenue to keep growing at the same pace, the linear trend is right. If growth is likely to slow down, for example because a tax base is nearing saturation, the damped trend is safer. It is a judgement, and it should be recorded in the working papers.

To include seasonality with \(m\) periods in a year (12 for monthly data) along with trend and level, Holt and Winters added a seasonal component \(s_t\) with its own smoothing parameter \(\gamma\). The equations of the Holt-Winters additive method are

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y}_{t+h|t} &= l_t + hb_t + s_{t+h-m(k+1)} \\ l_t &= \alpha(y_t - s_{t-m}) + (1-\alpha)(l_{t-1} + b_{t-1}) \\ b_t &= \beta(l_t - l_{t-1}) + (1-\beta)b_{t-1} \\ s_t &= \gamma(y_t - l_{t-1} - b_{t-1}) + (1-\gamma)s_{t-m} \end{aligned} \tag{46.8}\]

where \(k\) is the whole number part of \((h-1)/m\). The term \(s_{t+h-m(k+1)}\) looks complicated, but it only means “the seasonal value of the same month in the latest year of data”. So a forecast for next March uses the seasonal effect estimated for the latest March. In the multiplicative version, the seasonal effect multiplies the level and trend instead of being added.

To apply the Holt-Winters method in R, we use the function hw(). In the code below, we forecast the monthly expenditure with both additive and multiplicative seasonality. See Figure 46.10.

We do not always have to choose between SES, Holt and Holt-Winters ourselves. The function ets() (short for Error, Trend, Seasonal) tries all these methods, with and without damping, additive and multiplicative. It picks the best one automatically. We will use it in the audit use case.

Seasonal decomposition methods

Seasonal decomposition separates a time series into its components, like trend, seasonality and noise. This lets us study each component and its effect on the series separately. There are several methods. We will discuss two of them.

46.5.4 Classical seasonal decomposition

The first is the classical decomposition method. It assumes that the seasonal pattern repeats identically every year. Its steps are

  • Trend component. The trend is estimated with a centred moving average over one full season, a 12-month average for monthly data. As we saw in the moving averages section, such an average cancels out the seasonal ups and downs.
  • Seasonal component. The trend is removed from the series. What is left is averaged for each month (or quarter) across all the years. This gives one seasonal value for each month.
  • Residual component. The trend and seasonal components are removed from the original series. What remains is the residual.

Let us decompose the monthly expenditure to see its components. In R, we use the function decompose(). Since we know the seasonality of spend is multiplicative, we set type = "multiplicative" (the default is additive).

spend_dec <- decompose(spend, type = "multiplicative")

TipAudit tip

The financial rules of most governments discourage a rush of expenditure in the last months of the year. A March index above 2 means that the March of an average year carries twice the spending of an average month. Compute this index for each department, and take up the departments with the highest index for detailed checks of March vouchers.

46.5.5 Seasonal and trend decomposition using Loess2

STL (Seasonal and Trend decomposition using Loess) is a robust and flexible method for decomposing a time series into trend, seasonal and remainder components. Instead of simple averages, it uses Loess, a method of fitting smooth curves, to estimate both the trend and the seasonal component. It repeats the two steps a few times, each time improving both estimates. It has some advantages over classical decomposition.

  • It gives a trend for the whole series, including the first and last months.
  • The seasonal pattern may be allowed to change slowly over the years.
  • It is robust, meaning a few unusual values do not spoil the estimates much.

STL works only with additive components. So for a multiplicative series like spend, we first take its logarithm. In R, we use the function stl(). Setting s.window = "periodic" tells it to keep the seasonal pattern the same every year. Setting robust = TRUE asks it to resist unusual months. A plot of the STL decomposition is shown in Figure 46.12.

spend_stl <- stl(log(spend), s.window = "periodic", robust = TRUE)
spend_stl |>
  plot()

Model-based techniques

Model-based techniques fit a specific statistical model to the time series, and estimate its parameters from the data. They need more assumptions, but can give a more detailed understanding of the series. The most widely used are

  1. Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) models. These explain each value from the previous values of the series (the autoregressive part, of order \(p\)) and from the previous forecast errors (the moving average part, of order \(q\)). Many series, like those with a trend, do not have a constant level. ARIMA handles them by working on the differences between consecutive values (the integrated part, of order \(d\)). An ARIMA model is written as ARIMA(\(p\),\(d\),\(q\)).
  2. Seasonal ARIMA (SARIMA) models. These extend ARIMA to include seasonality, by also relating each value to the values of the same month in earlier years. They are written as ARIMA(\(p\),\(d\),\(q\))(\(P\),\(D\),\(Q\))\(_m\), where the second set of orders is for the seasonal part, and \(m\) is the number of periods in a season.

Choosing \(p\), \(d\), \(q\) and their seasonal versions by hand needs experience. Fortunately, the function auto.arima() from forecast tries many combinations and picks the best one. The argument lambda = 0 asks it to work on the logarithm of the series, as spend is multiplicative. The forecasts are converted back to the original scale automatically.

46.5.6 Which method to choose?

We have seen many methods. Which one forecasts best? The honest way to find out is to test them. We hide the last part of the data, forecast it with each method using only the earlier data, and compare the forecasts with what actually happened. The earlier part is called the training data, and the hidden part the test data.

Let us keep April 2014 to March 2022 of spend for training, and the last two financial years, 2022-23 and 2023-24, for testing. The function accuracy() compares a forecast with the actual values. It gives the error measures we met in the regression chapter (Chapter 37), like RMSE, MAE and MAPE.

train <- window(spend, end = c(2022, 3))
test  <- window(spend, start = c(2022, 4))

forecasts <- list(
  "Naive"                 = naive(train, h = 24),
  "Seasonal naive"        = snaive(train, h = 24),
  "Holt-Winters (mult.)"  = hw(train, h = 24, seasonal = "multiplicative"),
  "ETS (automatic)"       = forecast(ets(train), h = 24),
  "ARIMA (automatic)"     = forecast(auto.arima(train, lambda = 0), h = 24)
)

sapply(forecasts, \(f) accuracy(f, test)["Test set", c("RMSE", "MAE", "MAPE")]) |>

The naive forecast is off by 118.5% on average. It is badly wrong because the last month of the training data is a March. It repeats that March peak for every month of the next two years. NoteNo method is always best

The automatic choices of ets() and auto.arima() are made on the training data. They are good starting points, but they do not always win on the test data. Here the automatic ETS model came a little behind the multiplicative Holt-Winters method. On another series, the order may change. So it is good practice to try a few methods and compare them on test data, as we did here.

46.7 Audit use case, is this year’s revenue believable?

Let us now use forecasting the way an auditor would. Revenue collected by a government office usually follows a steady pattern of growth and seasonality. If we forecast this year’s revenue from the past years, and the actual revenue falls well below the forecast, we have a lead. There may be leakage of revenue, like undervaluation, short levy, or receipts not accounted for.

46.7.1 Our sample data

We create monthly stamp duty receipts of an imaginary sub-registrar office, in ₹ lakh, from April 2017 to March 2024. Receipts grow by about 8% every year. They dip in the monsoon months, rise in the festive months of October and November, and peak in March. We have built two events into the data.

  • In April and May 2020, receipts nearly stopped because of the COVID-19 lockdown. This is a genuine, known event.
  • From September 2023, receipts are about 22% lower than they should be. This is our planted case of leakage.

46.8 A suggested workflow

For any time series analysis in audit, we may follow these steps.

  1. Create the time series. Arrange the data by month or quarter, without gaps, and convert it with ts(), giving the correct start and frequency. A financial year series starts at c(year, 4).
  2. Plot it. Look at the trend, the seasonality and any unusual events with autoplot(), ggseasonplot() and ggsubseriesplot().
  3. Decide additive or multiplicative. If the seasonal swings grow with the level of the series, take the logarithm, or use multiplicative methods.
  4. Decompose the series with decompose() or stl(), to measure the seasonal pattern (like the March index) and see the trend clearly. Look at the largest remainders for unusual months.
  5. Clean known one-time events in the past with tsclean(), and record what was cleaned.
  6. Forecast the period under audit from the earlier data, with ets(), hw() or auto.arima(). Where possible, compare methods on test data with accuracy().
  7. Compare the actual values with the forecast and its prediction interval. Quantify the gap in rupees.
  8. Verify with records. Find out what changed, and examine the documents of the period.
ImportantRemember

A forecast is the auditor’s expectation, not the truth. A gap between the actual and the forecast is a lead, not a finding. There may be a genuine reason, like a change in tax rates, a court order or a slowdown in the market. Always find out the reason and examine the records before reporting.
