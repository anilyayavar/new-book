9  Protecting Personal Data

An audit party examining an old age pension scheme asks the district office for its list of beneficiaries. The file arrives on a pen drive. It has the name, date of birth, Aadhaar number, mobile number, bank account, caste category and disability status of every pensioner in the district. Within a week, copies of it sit on two laptops, in an email account and in a folder shared with the whole team. Nobody meant any harm. But if any one of those copies leaks, thousands of old people can be cheated, harassed or embarrassed, and the audit office will be the source of the leak.

Auditors receive such data all the time. Pay bills carry PAN and bank accounts. Scholarship lists carry caste. Health schemes carry diagnoses. We need this data to do our work. We also have a duty to protect it while we hold it, and to let go of it when the work is done.

In this chapter we learn practical ways to do this in R. We first sort the fields of a list by how much harm they can do. We then learn to mask numbers for display, to replace identifiers with hashes and tokens, and to see why a plain hash is not enough. We check a table for small groups of people who could be recognised before it goes into a report. Finally, we encrypt a file before it leaves our computer. Two audit use cases show the methods at work. In the first, two departments find the persons common to their lists without sending each other a single Aadhaar number. In the second, a district table is made safe for publication.

Section 8.6 used a hash to prove that a file has not changed. Here we use the same tool for a different purpose, to hide a value while keeping it usable.

9.1 Why it matters

Three reasons make the protection of personal data a part of the auditor’s job.

  • Duty of care. The people in our data did not choose to give it to us. They gave it to a government office to get a pension, a scholarship or a ration card. The audit office receives it only because the law allows audit to see the records. That trust passes to us with the data.
  • The law. India has a law on this subject, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. It covers personal data in digital form, that is, data about a person who can be identified from it. It places duties on whoever decides why and how such data is processed. In broad terms, data must be used only for a lawful purpose, kept safe with reasonable security safeguards, and not kept once the purpose is served unless another law requires it. A breach must be reported, and the penalties for failing to protect data can be heavy. The Act and the rules made under it are being brought into force in stages. The Aadhaar law separately restricts the display and publication of Aadhaar numbers. How these laws apply to audit work is a matter for the instructions of your own organisation. The duty of care does not depend on that answer.
  • Data minimisation. The safest data is data we never took. Ask only for the fields the audit needs. Drop the rest as soon as the data arrives. Delete the working copies when the retention period ends.
TipAudit tip

When writing the requisition for data, list the fields the audit needs and the reason for each. If the test is whether pensioners are alive and below an income limit, we may need the Aadhaar number for matching but not the caste or the mobile number. A short requisition protects the people in the data and also reduces the work of the auditee.

9.2 Our data, an old age pension list

The Social Welfare Department of an imaginary state pays an old age pension of ₹1,000 a month, and ₹1,500 to a person with a disability. Under the scheme rules of our example, a person who already draws a government service pension is not eligible. We have two lists.

  • oap, the old age pension list of five districts. For each beneficiary it has an ID, name, gender, date of birth, Aadhaar number, mobile number, bank account, IFSC, PIN code, district, social category, disability status and monthly pension.
  • svc, the list of service pensioners kept by the Treasury. It has the pension payment order (PPO) number, name, date of birth, Aadhaar number, PAN and monthly pension.

The code below creates both lists and plants the cases we will find.

  • Seven persons are on both lists. In the Treasury list their Aadhaar numbers are written with spaces or hyphens, and one has two digits swapped by a typing error.
  • Hillpur is a small hill district with few beneficiaries, most of them from the Scheduled Tribes. A few beneficiaries are more than 90 years old.
  • All mobile numbers come from one series, so that we can show how a plain hash is broken.
Note

Everything in this chapter is simulated. The names are made up. The Aadhaar numbers are random 12 digit strings and the PAN are random strings in the PAN pattern. Mobile numbers, bank accounts, IFSC and PIN codes are invented too. Because the data is simulated, we print it in full in this section so that the reader can see what we are protecting. With real data, we would not print identifiers on screen or in a report at all.

9.3 Sorting the fields

Not every column carries the same risk. Before deciding how to protect a list, we sort its columns into three kinds.

  • Direct identifiers point to one person by themselves. Aadhaar number, PAN, mobile number, bank account and, in practice, the full name are of this kind.
  • Quasi-identifiers do not identify anyone alone. Combined, they often do. Date of birth, gender and PIN code are the classic three. A person may be the only woman born on a given date in a given PIN code.
  • Sensitive attributes are the facts that harm or embarrass a person if they become known. Caste category, disability, health condition, religion and income are of this kind.

Table 9.1 sorts the columns of our two lists.

It helps to write this sorting into the code. Then every later step can use it, and a reviewer can see what was decided.

direct_ids <- c("name", "aadhaar", "pan", "mobile", "bank_account")
quasi_ids  <- c("dob", "gender", "pin_code", "district", "ifsc")
sensitive  <- c("category", "disability", "monthly_pension")

Data minimisation now takes one line. Suppose a test needs only the district, gender, date of birth, category, disability and pension. We keep those, with the beneficiary ID to trace records back, and drop everything else.

oap_min <- oap |>
  select(ben_id, district, gender, dob, category, disability, monthly_pension)

ncol(oap)
[1] 13
ncol(oap_min)
[1] 7

The working copy now has 7 columns instead of 13. Aadhaar, mobile and bank account are no longer in it. If this copy leaks, far less harm is done.

9.4 Masking numbers for display

Often we must show which record we mean, in a working paper or an audit memo, without showing the whole number. Masking replaces most of the characters with X and keeps a few, usually the last four. The masked Aadhaar issued by UIDAI follows the same idea and shows only the last four digits.

The stringr functions we learnt in Chapter 20 do this. str_sub(x, -4) takes the last four characters of a string.

x <- "987654321098"
str_sub(x, -4)
[1] "1098"
paste0("XXXX-XXXX-", str_sub(x, -4))
[1] "XXXX-XXXX-1098"

Let us write small functions for the identifiers in our data. Bank account numbers have different lengths, so we make as many X as needed with str_dup(). For PAN, str_replace() replaces the first five letters, and the regular expression ^[A-Z]{5} matches exactly those (Chapter 21).

mask_aadhaar <- function(x) paste0("XXXX-XXXX-", str_sub(x, -4))
mask_mobile  <- function(x) paste0("XXXXXX", str_sub(x, -4))
mask_account <- function(x) paste0(str_dup("X", str_length(x) - 4), str_sub(x, -4))
mask_pan     <- function(x) str_replace(x, "^[A-Z]{5}", "XXXXX")

oap |>
  transmute(ben_id, name,
            aadhaar      = mask_aadhaar(aadhaar),
            mobile       = mask_mobile(mobile),
            bank_account = mask_account(bank_account)) |>
  head(4)

[1] "XXXXX6557E" "XXXXX8516V" "XXXXX8888W"

This is the form in which identifiers should appear in a working paper, an audit memo or a draft paragraph sent to the department.

Masking is meant for human eyes. It is not a way to hide a list from a determined person, and it destroys information. Many people share the same last four digits. Let us count how often that happens in our small list.

oap |>
  count(last4 = str_sub(aadhaar, -4)) |>
  filter(n > 1) |>
  nrow()
[1] 20

Even among 600 persons, 20 endings of four digits are shared by two or more people. So masked numbers cannot be used to match two lists. For matching, we need hashing.

WarningCaution

A masked number is still part of a record. “XXXX-XXXX-1234, Kamla Devi, Hillpur” may be enough to find the person. Mask the identifier, and also ask whether the name and place need to appear at all.

9.5 Hashing for pseudonymisation

A hash turns any text into a fixed length code. The same input always gives the same code, and the code gives no visible clue about the input. Section 8.6 used the MD5 hash of a whole file. For identifiers we use a stronger hash called SHA-256. Replacing an identifier by such a code is called pseudonymisation. The records stay linked to each other, but the identifier itself is hidden.

The digest package hashes one value at a time.

digest("987654321098", algo = "sha256", serialize = FALSE)
[1] "f1baafb72e1cc8b1f857f9ed9f448249a7c5ee5116fe4dbbe69823744e3dc940"
WarningCaution

Always write serialize = FALSE when hashing a value with digest(). Without it, digest() first converts the value into R’s own internal format and hashes that. The result is a valid hash, but it will not agree with the hash of the same number made by any other tool, or by another department.

For a whole column, the sha256() function of openssl is more convenient, because it hashes every element of a vector at once. It gives the same result as digest() above.

as.character(sha256("987654321098"))
[1] "f1baafb72e1cc8b1f857f9ed9f448249a7c5ee5116fe4dbbe69823744e3dc940"

Two small numbers that differ in one digit get codes that have nothing in common.

as.character(sha256(c("987654321098", "987654321099")))
[1] "f1baafb72e1cc8b1f857f9ed9f448249a7c5ee5116fe4dbbe69823744e3dc940"
[2] "6f42d301bbabdd248689eb74b9719481c70fbe52853cef63b305cdf9e5f044df"

9.5.1 Normalise before hashing

To a hash function, "9876 5432 1098" and "987654321098" are two different texts, and their hashes differ completely. So before hashing we must write every identifier in one standard form. For Aadhaar, we keep only the digits with str_remove_all() and the pattern \\D, which means any character that is not a digit.

normalise_id <- function(x) str_remove_all(x, "\\D")

normalise_id(c("9876 5432 1098", "9876-5432-1098", "987654321098"))
[1] "987654321098" "987654321098" "987654321098"

All three are now the same text, and will get the same hash. For PAN, we would also convert to upper case with str_to_upper().

9.5.2 Why a plain hash is not enough

A hash cannot be run backwards. But it does not need to be. If an outsider knows what kind of value was hashed, the outsider can hash every possible value and look up the answers. This is called a dictionary attack. It works whenever the number of possible values is small enough for a computer to try them all.

Let us see it happen. Suppose the department shares a list in which the mobile numbers have been replaced by their plain SHA-256 hashes. It looks safe.

shared <- oap |>
  transmute(ben_id, mobile_hash = as.character(sha256(mobile)))

head(shared, 3)
# A tibble: 3 × 2
  ben_id   mobile_hash                                                     
  <chr>    <chr>                                                           
1 OAP00001 516711e68278cd3befaa34d99df50753308687efb33ae4a424f2b3654d772853
2 OAP00002 f99f5c16eaa209817362ccf6d0fa21d74769866135fecbf282bcd3cac3d0c18c
3 OAP00003 92f01112ef0b5bb0f302568338a46d6ff1772134eff1aa4ecc8fa9d4e660b06e

Now suppose someone who receives this list knows, or guesses, that the numbers come from the series 70000 00000 to 70000 99999. That is only one lakh numbers. The receiver hashes all of them and keeps a table of number and hash.

attack_time <- system.time({
  all_numbers <- paste0("70000", sprintf("%05d", 0:99999))
  dictionary  <- tibble(mobile      = all_numbers,
                        mobile_hash = as.character(sha256(all_numbers)))
})

recovered <- shared |>
  inner_join(dictionary, by = "mobile_hash")

nrow(recovered)
[1] 600

Every one of the 600 mobile numbers has been recovered. On this computer, building the dictionary took about 0.2 seconds.

Real identifiers come from larger sets. There are 1010 possible ten digit numbers, and about 1012 possible Aadhaar numbers. But that is not much protection either. At the speed we just saw, R alone would try all 1010 numbers in about 6 hours. Specialised hardware is very much faster. So a plain hash of a phone number, Aadhaar number, PAN or bank account must be treated as if it were the number itself.

9.5.3 The fix, a keyed hash

The attack worked because anyone can compute SHA-256. The fix is to mix a secret key into the hash, so that only a holder of the key can compute it. This is called a keyed hash, or HMAC (hash-based message authentication code). In openssl, we just add the argument key to sha256().

A good key is long and random. rand_bytes() gives random bytes, and paste() turns them into a text of hexadecimal characters.

secret_key <- paste(rand_bytes(32), collapse = "")
nchar(secret_key)
[1] 64

The key is printed here only to show its length. In real work, the key is never printed, never stored next to the data, and never written in the script itself. We return to this below.

Now we hash the mobile numbers with the key, and try the same attack.

shared_keyed <- oap |>
  transmute(ben_id, mobile_hash = as.character(sha256(mobile, key = secret_key)))

shared_keyed |>
  inner_join(dictionary, by = "mobile_hash") |>
  nrow()
[1] 0

Nothing is recovered. The outsider can still hash every number in the series, but without the key those hashes never agree with ours. Only someone who has the key can repeat the attack. So the protection lies entirely in keeping the key secret and apart from the data.

digest has a function for the same purpose, hmac(). It takes the key first, and gives the same code as openssl.

hmac(secret_key, "7000012345", algo = "sha256") ==
  as.character(sha256("7000012345", key = secret_key))
[1] TRUE

9.5.4 Salt and key, in simple words

Two words come up whenever hashing is discussed.

  • A salt is a random value made separately for each record and stored next to the hash. Two persons with the same number get different hashes, because their salts differ. Salts are used for stored passwords.
  • A key, sometimes called a pepper, is one secret value used for every record and kept away from the data.

For audit work, the key is usually what we want. A salt has two drawbacks for us. First, because it is stored with the data, anyone who gets the data also gets the salt, and can still try every possible number for any one record. Second, because the same number gets a different hash in each record, two lists hashed with salts can no longer be matched. A small example shows this.

salted_hash <- function(x) {
  salt <- map_chr(seq_along(x), \(i) paste(rand_bytes(8), collapse = ""))
  tibble(salt = salt, hash = as.character(sha256(paste0(salt, x))))
}

9.6 Audit use case, matching two lists without sharing Aadhaar

The audit wants to know which old age pensioners also draw a service pension, since such persons are not eligible under our scheme rules. The usual way would be to collect both lists, with Aadhaar numbers, on the auditor’s laptop. A safer way keeps every Aadhaar number inside the department that already holds it.

  1. The audit office makes one secret key and gives it, in a sealed form or by a secure channel, to one officer in each department.
  2. Each department normalises its Aadhaar numbers and hashes them with the key.
  3. Each department sends only its own record ID and the hash. No names, no numbers.
  4. The auditor joins the two hashed lists. A match means the same Aadhaar number.
  5. The auditor sends back only the matched record IDs, and asks each department for the full records of those persons alone.

We write one function for step 2, so that both departments do exactly the same thing.

keyed_hash <- function(x, key) {
  as.character(sha256(normalise_id(x), key = key))
}

Each department now prepares its file. Note that the Treasury’s numbers have spaces and hyphens in them. normalise_id() takes care of that.

from_welfare <- oap |>
  transmute(ben_id, aadhaar_hash = keyed_hash(aadhaar, secret_key))

from_treasury <- svc |>

TipAudit tip

9.7 Tokens

A hash is calculated from the identifier. A token is not. It is a random code given to each person, and the only link between the token and the person is a lookup table. Without the table, the token reveals nothing at all, even to someone who tries every possible number.

Tokens suit data that stays with the audit team for a long analysis. We replace the identifiers by tokens in the working copy, and keep the lookup table separately, under lock.

lookup <- oap |>
  distinct(ben_id, aadhaar) |>
  mutate(token = map_chr(ben_id, \(i) toupper(paste(rand_bytes(5), collapse = ""))))

n_distinct(lookup$token) == nrow(lookup)
[1] TRUE

Each token is ten random characters, and every token is different. The working copy keeps only the token, with no direct identifiers.

oap_work <- oap |>
  left_join(lookup |> select(ben_id, token), by = "ben_id") |>
  select(token, district, gender, dob, category, disability, monthly_pension)

1 B1C33A2B46 Sonpur   M      1958-11-09 SC       No                    1000

When a case needs to be traced back to the person, the officer who holds lookup finds the record. Nobody else needs it. Tokens made with rand_bytes() are truly random. Do not make tokens with sample() after set.seed(), because anyone who knows the seed and the order of the records can make the same tokens again.

Note

A hash is the right tool when two parties must match their lists without meeting, because both can compute the same code from the same number. A token is the right tool inside one team, because it cannot be computed by anyone, and can be undone only with the lookup table.

9.8 Generalisation and k-anonymity

Removing names and numbers is not enough. Quasi-identifiers can single out a person by themselves. Let us count how many people in our list share each combination of gender, date of birth and PIN code.

oap |>
  count(gender, dob, pin_code) |>
  count(n, name = "groups")
# A tibble: 2 × 2
      n groups
  <int>  <int>
1     1    596
2     2      2

Of the 598 combinations, 596 belong to exactly one person. So anyone who knows a neighbour’s date of birth and PIN code can find her in the list, and read her caste and disability status. Removing the Aadhaar number did not help her at all.

A list is called k-anonymous if every combination of quasi-identifiers is shared by at least k persons. Then no record can be narrowed down to fewer than k people. A common choice is k = 5.

We reach k-anonymity by generalisation, that is, by making the quasi-identifiers coarser. Instead of the date of birth, we give the age band. Instead of the PIN code, we give the district.

oap_gen <- oap |>
  mutate(
    age = floor(as.numeric(as.Date("2025-03-31") - dob) / 365.25),
    age_band = cut(age, breaks = c(60, 70, 80, 90, Inf), right = FALSE,
                   labels = c("60-69", "70-79", "80-89", "90+"))
  ) |>
  select(district, gender, age_band, category, disability)

Now we check whether any combination has fewer than k persons. count() gives the size of each group, and filter() keeps the small ones.

k <- 5

small_groups <- oap_gen |>

Look also at the sensitive columns within each small group before sharing.

9.9 Audit use case, publishing a district table safely

The draft audit report is to include a table of old age pensioners by district and social category. The table has no names. Can it still harm anyone? It can, through small numbers. If a cell says that one person in Hillpur is from the General category, everyone in Hillpur may know who that is, and the next table may then reveal her disability or pension.

The usual rule is small-cell suppression. Any count below a threshold, often 5, is shown as “less than 5” instead of the number. Let us make the table first.

tab <- oap |>
  count(district, category) |>
  complete(district, category, fill = list(n = 0))

tab |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = category, values_from = n)
# A tibble: 5 × 5
  district  General   OBC    SC    ST
  <chr>       <int> <int> <int> <int>
1 Bhimnagar      34    53    18     5
2 Hillpur         1     0     4    35
3 Nandgaon       49    55    22     4
4 Rajgarh        43    66    33     8
5 Sonpur         52    82    31     5

In all, 4 cells are below 5, and 3 of them are in Hillpur. One cell is zero. A zero is also below 5, so we hide it too. First we mark the cells to hide.

threshold <- 5

tab_safe <- tab |>
  mutate(suppress = n < threshold)

There is one more trap. If the table shows a row total, a single hidden cell can be worked out by subtraction. So, in every row with exactly one hidden cell, we hide the next smallest cell too. This is called secondary suppression.

tab_safe <- tab_safe |>
  mutate(
    hidden_in_row = sum(suppress),
    order_in_row  = rank(n, ties.method = "first"),
    suppress      = suppress | (hidden_in_row == 1 & order_in_row == 2),
    .by = district
  )

tab_safe |>
  mutate(shown = case_when(n < threshold ~ "less than 5",
                           suppress      ~ "not shown",
                           .default      = as.character(n))) |>
  select(district, category, shown) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = category, values_from = shown) |>
  left_join(count(oap, district, name = "Total"), by = "district")

  <chr>     <chr>       <chr>       <chr>       <chr>       <int>

2 Hillpur   less than 5 less than 5 less than 5 35             40
3 Nandgaon  49          55          not shown   less than 5   130
4 Rajgarh   43          66          33          8             150

The table now hides 5 cells. The SC cell of Nandgaon, with 22 persons, is hidden only to protect the ST cell beside it. We show it as “not shown” and not as “less than 5”, so that the table never says anything untrue. A footnote in the report should explain both labels.

Column totals need the same check. A simpler way out, often good enough in an audit report, is to merge small categories into “Others”, or small districts with a neighbour, before making the table.

What the auditor does next is to read the safe table once more as an outsider would. Could anyone in the district guess who is behind any number? If yes, coarsen it further. Then put the code and the threshold used in the working papers, so that the reviewer can see how the table was made safe.

TipAudit tip

Apply the same check to charts. A bar for a group of one or two persons in a chart discloses as much as a cell in a table.

9.10 Encrypting files before sharing

Sometimes the full data must travel, by email or on a pen drive. Then the file should be encrypted. An encrypted file looks like random bytes. Only someone with the right key can turn it back into the original.

We will use AES, the standard method used by governments and banks around the world, through the openssl package. AES needs a key of exactly 32 random bytes, which no person can remember. So we let the person remember a passphrase, and turn the passphrase into a key with bcrypt_pbkdf(). This function is deliberately slow. That costs us a fraction of a second, but makes it very slow for an outsider to try millions of guesses at the passphrase. It also takes a salt, a few random bytes, so that the same passphrase gives a different key for every file. The salt is not secret, and we store it at the start of the encrypted file.

Two small functions do the work. readBin() and writeBin() read and write a file as raw bytes.

encrypt_file <- function(infile, outfile, passphrase) {
  salt   <- rand_bytes(16)
  key    <- bcrypt_pbkdf(passphrase, salt, rounds = 64L, size = 32L)
  plain  <- readBin(infile, "raw", file.size(infile))
  locked <- aes_cbc_encrypt(plain, key = key)
  writeBin(c(salt, attr(locked, "iv"), locked), outfile)
}

decrypt_file <- function(infile, outfile, passphrase) {
  bytes  <- readBin(infile, "raw", file.size(infile))
  salt   <- bytes[1:16]
  iv     <- bytes[17:32]
  key    <- bcrypt_pbkdf(passphrase, salt, rounds = 64L, size = 32L)
  plain  <- aes_cbc_decrypt(bytes[-(1:32)], key = key, iv = iv)
  writeBin(plain, outfile)
}

The iv is another set of random bytes that AES needs. Like the salt, it is not secret, so we store it in the file too.

Let us save the list of matched persons to a CSV file in a temporary folder, and encrypt it. We also record its hash, as in Section 8.6, using digest() with file = TRUE.

write_csv(found, csv_file)

9.10.1 Keys and passphrases

The encryption is only as strong as the care taken with the passphrase.

  • Make it long. Four or five unrelated words with a number are easier to remember and much harder to guess than a short word with symbols.
  • Never write it in the script. We wrote it in the code above only to show how it works. In real work, type it when needed. askpass() from openssl opens a box in which the passphrase is typed without being shown, as in passphrase <- askpass("Passphrase for the file").
  • Send it by a different channel. If the file goes by email, give the passphrase by a phone call or a text message, never in the same email or a second email. Anyone who can read the mailbox would otherwise have both.
  • Keep the secret keys of hashing the same way. The key used for matching in Section 9.6 must never travel with the hashed lists.
  • Do not lose it. There is no way to recover an encrypted file without its passphrase. Keep it as your organisation’s instructions say.
Note

The receiver may not use R. In that case, use an archive tool that supports AES-256 encryption, such as 7-Zip, and choose AES-256 in its settings. The older “ZIP password” method is weak and should not be used. The rules about the passphrase are the same.

9.11 What never to do

Most leaks do not come from clever attacks. They come from ordinary habits. With audit data, never do any of the following.

  • Upload it to online tools. This includes websites that convert PDFs or merge spreadsheets, online chart makers and AI chatbots. Once uploaded, the data is on someone else’s computer, under someone else’s terms, and cannot be called back. If you need help with code, describe the columns or share a few made-up rows, never the real data.
  • Send it from a personal email account, or through a personal messaging app. Use the official email, and encrypt the file.
  • Leave copies about. This means copies on a personal laptop or phone, a shared computer, a pen drive left in a drawer, or the Downloads folder. Keep one working copy in the audit project folder, on an official machine.
  • Keep it after the retention period. When the period laid down by your organisation ends, delete the data and its copies, including the encrypted ones and the backups.
  • Print identifiers when a masked form will do. A working paper rarely needs the full Aadhaar number.
WarningCaution

Hashed, tokenised and generalised data is still personal data. Whoever holds the key or the lookup table can link it back to the person, and the law covers data about anyone who can be identified. These methods reduce the risk, and the harm if a leak happens. They do not remove the duty of care. Pseudonymised data must still be kept securely, used only for the audit, and deleted on time.

9.12 A suggested workflow

  1. Ask for less. List in the requisition only the fields the audit needs, with the reason for each.
  2. Sort the fields. Mark each column as a direct identifier, a quasi-identifier or a sensitive attribute, and write this in the code.
  3. Minimise on arrival. Record the file hash as in Section 8.6, then make a working copy without the fields the analysis does not need.
  4. Pseudonymise. Replace identifiers with tokens for work inside the team, or with keyed hashes when two parties must match lists. Never use a plain hash.
  5. Keep secrets apart. Store the key, the lookup table and the passphrases away from the data, and destroy matching keys after use.
  6. Mask for display. Show only the last four digits of identifiers in working papers and memos.
  7. Check before publishing. Generalise quasi-identifiers, check k-anonymity with count(), and suppress small cells, with secondary suppression where totals are shown.
  8. Encrypt before sending. Encrypt any file that leaves the official machine, and send the passphrase by a different channel.
  9. Delete on time. Remove all copies when the retention period ends.
ImportantRemember
  • Take and keep only the data the audit needs.
  • A plain hash of a number from a small set can be reversed by trying every value. Use a keyed hash, and keep the key apart from the data.
  • Masking is for display, hashing is for matching, tokens are for working, encryption is for travelling.
  • A table with no names can still identify people through small cells.
  • Pseudonymised data is still personal data. The duty of care stays with the auditor.
