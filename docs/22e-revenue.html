66  Revenue and Tax

Last updated

23 October 2026

A State taxes the supply of goods and services. About two thousand dealers in eight districts file a return every month. Each return declares the dealer’s sales, the tax due on them, and the credit claimed for tax already paid on purchases. The dealer pays the rest. Nobody checks most returns in detail. So a dealer may declare less than was sold, charge an old lower rate, or claim a refund that is not due. A dealer may stop filing and carry on trading. A shell firm may exist only to pass on credit.

Most chapters of this part look for money wrongly spent. Revenue audit looks for money not collected. It asks two questions. Was all the tax that should have been assessed actually assessed and collected? Were exemptions, concessions and refunds allowed correctly? The loss is harder to see, because nothing was paid out. It shows only when the return is read against another record, such as the movement of goods, the buyer’s own return or the guideline value of a property.

Earlier chapters already hold several revenue tests, such as the credit reconciliation of Section 60.5, circular trading in Section 55.4, and refund models in Chapter 41. This chapter runs them together for one department, in a few lines each, and adds what the book lacks. These are tests of registration and filing, of tax declared against third-party data, and of rates, thresholds and refunds. Others look for shell dealers, check stamp duty on property, and age the demands not collected. It then combines the flags for each dealer, and follows two cases to the end.

A flag is not a finding. Many flags in tax data have an innocent cause, such as a seasonal trade, an export or a genuine closure. The aim is to find the few that need a question.

66.1 Where revenue leaks

A tax on supplies rests on a few records. Each can be read against the others.

The controls are simple. Every registered dealer should file every return. What a dealer declares should agree with what third parties record. Tax should be charged at the rate in force on the date of supply. A concession or a refund should go only to those entitled to it. A demand should be collected before the time to recover it runs out.

66.2 Our data, a year of a State tax on goods and services

Our auditee is the tax department of an imaginary State, with eight districts. We have its records for 2024-25. All names and numbers are made up. There are twelve tables.

  • dealers is the registration master. It holds the dealer ID, name, a PAN-like code, trade, district, address, dates of registration and cancellation, scheme, bank account and mobile.
  • returns has one row per dealer per month filed. It holds turnover, exports, exempt sales, taxable value at 5%, 12% and 18%, output tax, input tax credit (ITC) claimed, tax paid and the filing date.
  • movement summarises the movement records of goods sent by each dealer each month.
  • buyer_reports holds the purchases each buyer reported from each supplier each month.
  • treasury holds the tax credited to the State’s account, by return month.
  • rate_master gives the rate for each commodity code from each date. supply_lines holds the invoices of forty dealers of building material, with the rate charged.
  • refunds holds the refund claims, with dates, amounts and the bank account credited.
  • demands holds the tax demands raised after scrutiny, back to 2017, and collections the amounts recovered.
  • deeds holds about three thousand property documents registered in one sub-registrar office, and guideline the guideline rate per square metre for each locality and type of property.

The tax follows a few example norms. Most goods are taxed at 5%, 12% or 18%. A retailer with a yearly turnover up to ₹1.5 crore may choose a composition scheme, paying a flat 1% of turnover with no credit. In our example the scheme is closed to dealers of iron and steel and of electronics. A return is due by the 20th of the next month. The rate on ceramic tiles rose from 12% to 18% on 1 October 2024. Stamp duty is 6% of the declared value or the guideline value, whichever is higher. A demand must be recovered within five years of being raised. These are illustrative rules for this example only. In real work, apply the actual law, rates and time limits of your own jurisdiction.

Most of the data is routine. Fertiliser sales rise and fall with the sowing seasons, and some small fertiliser dealers sell nothing in winter. Some dealers closed during the year and had their registrations cancelled. A few dealers are dormant and file nil returns. Some exporters receive large refunds of credit, as the law intends. Some property documents valued below the guideline value were referred for valuation, as the rules allow. Into this, these cases have been planted.

  1. Nil returns while goods move. A steel wholesaler files nil returns from September while its goods keep moving.
  2. Turnover hidden. An electronics wholesaler declares about a third of its sales, in round lakh, while its buyers report far more.
  3. A sharp fall. A cement dealer’s tax falls sharply from October while the trade grows.
  4. An old rate. A building material dealer keeps charging 12% on tiles after the rate rose to 18%.
  5. Bunching. Two dozen retailers keep their turnover just under the composition limit, and three dealers in excluded trades use the scheme.
  6. Refunds to new linked dealers. Three “exporters” registered in November share a bank account and a mobile, and receive refunds within days.
  7. A refund above all tax paid. A dealer registered in August is refunded more than all the tax it ever paid.
  8. A ring of shell dealers. Four dealers registered in June trade goods round in a circle, and stop filing after January.
  9. Stamp duty. Documents in one locality are valued far below the guideline value. One property is sold twice by the same seller. Shops are registered as houses.
  10. Old demands. Large demands of 2020-21 are uncollected and close to the time limit.

66.5 Tax declared against third-party data

A dealer writes its own return. Two other records describe the same trade, and the dealer does not control them. The movement records show goods sent. The buyers’ reports show what others say they bought from the dealer. Neither should be much larger than the turnover declared.

66.15.1 What the auditor does next

  1. Ask for the refund files. Who sanctioned each refund, what was checked, and why so fast? Were export documents such as shipping bills on file and verified?
  2. Ask for the registering officer’s file of all seven dealers. Who verified the premises and the identity of the proprietors, and when?
  3. Ask the bank, through the department, for the account holder and the statement of the refund account. Where did the money go next?
  4. Arrange a physical verification of the business premises of all seven at the addresses given, with the local officer.
  5. If confirmed, the department should recover the refunds, block the credit passed by the ring, and cancel the registrations. Report the case, and the control weakness of refunds sanctioned without a look at the claimant’s age, suppliers and identifiers.

66.16 A suggested workflow

  1. Get the full data, including movement records, buyers’ reports, the rate master, refund and demand registers, and the property documents. Record who extracted it and when.
  2. Check completeness. A unique key, every return linked to a dealer, and tax paid agreeing with the treasury.
  3. Test filing. Returns expected against received, dealers who stopped, nil returns, late returns, and cancelled dealers still trading.
  4. Compare declarations with third-party data. Movement records, buyers’ reports and the trend of the trade.
  5. Check rates, limits and concessions. The rate in force, bunching below a limit, and eligibility for a scheme.
  6. Test refunds. New claimants, shared accounts, fast sanctions, and refunds above the tax or credit.
  7. Look for shell dealers. Shared identifiers, short lives and circles of trade.
  8. Test stamp duty and demands. Guideline values, duplicates, property type, and the age of open demands.
  9. Add the tests of earlier chapters, combine the flags for each dealer, and follow up the top cases on paper and in person.
ImportantRemember
  • Revenue audit looks for money not collected. The loss shows only when a return is read against another record.
  • Count the returns expected against those received. A dealer who stops filing, or files nil, while goods move is still trading.
  • Movement records and buyers’ reports put a floor under a dealer’s sales. Compare them over a year, to allow for timing.
  • Use a rolling join to find the rate in force on each date.
  • Concessions with limits invite bunching just below them. A histogram shows it.
  • Refunds turn credit into cash. Look at the claimant’s age, account, suppliers and the speed of sanction.
  • Many flags have innocent causes. Several flags on one dealer deserve the auditor’s time.
