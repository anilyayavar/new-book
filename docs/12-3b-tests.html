28  Statistical Tests for Auditors

An auditor finds that one office approves 91 out of every 100 medical claims, while the rest of the department approves 84. Is that office lax in its checks, or is the difference just the kind of variation we should expect between any two offices? One dealing officer’s claims are larger on average than everyone else’s. Is that a pattern worth an inquiry, or a coincidence?

These are the questions a statistical test answers. It tells us whether a difference we see is larger than chance alone would usually produce. A test does not prove an irregularity. But it helps us decide where to spend our limited audit time, and it protects us from two embarrassments: chasing differences that mean nothing, and missing ones that matter.

In this chapter we learn the idea of a test in plain words, and then the tests an auditor needs most often. Each answers a question that comes up in real audits.

Prerequisites

library(tidyverse)
library(broom)   # turns test results into tidy tables

All the tests themselves come with base R, in the package stats, which is always loaded.

28.1 The idea of a test

Every test follows the same reasoning, which is very close to how an auditor already thinks.

  1. Start by assuming nothing is unusual. This assumption is called the null hypothesis. For example, “this office approves claims at the same rate as the others”. It is like presuming an auditee innocent until the evidence says otherwise.
  2. Ask how surprising the data would be, if nothing were unusual. The answer is the p-value, a probability between 0 and 1. A p-value of 0.30 means that, if nothing were unusual, a difference at least this large would turn up 30% of the time by chance alone. Not surprising at all. A p-value of 0.001 means it would turn up only once in a thousand times. Very surprising.
  3. Decide. If the p-value is small, below a limit we fix in advance, we conclude that chance is an unlikely explanation, and call the difference statistically significant. The usual limit is 0.05, or 5%.

A test can go wrong in two ways, and both have an audit meaning.

The 5% limit is the chance of a false alarm we are willing to accept. A smaller limit means fewer false alarms but more misses. With a small sample, a test may miss even a large difference, as we will see.

NoteWhat if we have the whole population?

Auditors often analyse 100% of the data, not a sample. If one office approved 94% of its claims and another 86%, that difference is a fact. Why test it? Because claims are approved or rejected for many reasons, and even two identical offices would not show identical rates in a year. The test asks whether the difference is larger than this ordinary variation in the process. So tests are useful on full data too, as long as we read “significant” as “unlikely to be ordinary variation”, and not as a finding in itself.

28.2 Our data

We use one data set through most of the chapter. It is a year’s register of medical reimbursement claims of a department with six offices, each office with two dealing officers. For each claim we have the amount, whether it was approved, and the days taken to settle it.

28.3 Is the error rate above the tolerable rate?

Let us start with a question from a test of controls. The auditor checks a random sample of 60 claims, and finds 6 settled without the required verification. The tolerable deviation rate is 5%. The sample’s rate is 10%, twice the tolerable rate. Can we conclude that the control is failing?

binom.test() compares a count of “successes” (here, deviations) in a number of trials with an expected proportion. Since we only care whether the rate is above 5%, we use alternative = "greater".

dev_test <- binom.test(x = 6, n = 60, p = 0.05, alternative = "greater")
dev_test

    Exact binomial test

data:  6 and 60
number of successes = 6, number of trials = 60, p-value = 0.07872
alternative hypothesis: true probability of success is greater than 0.05
95 percent confidence interval:
 0.04445297 1.00000000
sample estimates:
probability of success 
                   0.1

The p-value is 0.079, above 0.05. Surprisingly, a sample of 60 with 6 deviations is not enough to conclude that the true rate exceeds 5%. With 60 claims, a true rate of 5% would give 6 or more deviations about 8% of the time. The confidence interval says the same: the true rate could be as low as 4.4%.

What if the auditor had found 9 deviations instead?

binom.test(x = 9, n = 60, p = 0.05, alternative = "greater")$p.value
[1] 0.002848606

Now the conclusion is clear. This is the reason why sample sizes for tests of controls are planned in advance, as we did in Section 27.7. A test on a sample too small to detect the difference we care about can only end in “not proven”.

Caution

“Not significant” does not mean “no problem”. It means the data are not strong enough to rule out chance. Never report a control as effective only because a test was not significant, least of all on a small sample. Report what was found, 6 deviations in 60, and the limit of what the sample can tell.

28.4 Comparing two rates

Back to the full register. Rampur approves more claims than the rest of the department. Is the difference more than chance?

claims |>
  summarise(claims = n(), n_approved = sum(approved),
            approval_rate = round(mean(approved), 3),
            .by = office) |>
  arrange(desc(approval_rate))
# A tibble: 6 × 4
  office   claims n_approved approval_rate
  <chr>     <int>      <int>         <dbl>
1 Rampur      433        395         0.912
2 Fatehpur    445        381         0.856
3 Belapur     527        445         0.844
4 Amarpur     587        495         0.843
5 Chandpur    523        440         0.841

prop.test() compares two (or more) proportions. We give it the number approved and the number of claims in each group.

rampur <- claims$office == "Rampur"
rampur_test <- prop.test(x = c(sum(claims$approved[rampur]), sum(claims$approved[!rampur])),
                         n = c(sum(rampur), sum(!rampur)))
rampur_test

    2-sample test for equality of proportions with continuity correction

data:  c(sum(claims$approved[rampur]), sum(claims$approved[!rampur])) out of c(sum(rampur), sum(!rampur))
X-squared = 13.355, df = 1, p-value = 0.0002577
alternative hypothesis: two.sided
95 percent confidence interval:
 0.03697932 0.09992800
sample estimates:
   prop 1    prop 2 
0.9122402 0.8437865

Rampur approved 91.2% of its claims, against 84.4% elsewhere. The p-value is 0.00026, far below 0.05. A difference this large would almost never arise by chance. The confidence interval says that Rampur’s rate is higher by somewhere between 3.7 and 10 percentage points.

28.5 Comparing many groups at once

Rampur caught our eye because we looked at the table first. A fairer way is to test all six offices together, without picking one in advance. The chi-square test of independence checks whether two categorical variables, here office and approval, are related. If approval did not depend on the office, every office would approve about the same share.

approval_table <- table(claims$office, claims$approved)
approval_table
          
           FALSE TRUE
  Amarpur     92  495
  Belapur     82  445
  Chandpur    83  440
  Devgarh     80  405
  Fatehpur    64  381
  Rampur      38  395
office_test <- chisq.test(approval_table)
office_test

    Pearson's Chi-squared test

data:  approval_table
X-squared = 14.77, df = 5, p-value = 0.01139

The p-value of 0.011 is below 0.05, so approval rates do differ between offices. But the test does not say which office is different. For that, look at the standardised residuals. Each says how far an office’s count is from what we would expect if all offices were alike, in units of standard deviation. Values beyond about 2 (or below -2) deserve attention.

round(office_test$stdres, 2)
          
           FALSE  TRUE
  Amarpur   0.79 -0.79
  Belapur   0.66 -0.66
  Chandpur  0.88 -0.88
  Devgarh   1.27 -1.27
  Fatehpur -0.16  0.16
  Rampur   -3.73  3.73

Only Rampur stands out, with 3.7 standard deviations more approvals than expected. Every other office is within 2.

TipAudit tip

The chi-square test with its residuals is one of the most useful tests in audit. It works on any cross-table of counts: rejections by inspector, tenders won by bidder and department, cases disposed of by officer and type, or exceptions by branch. The residuals point straight at the cells which are out of line.

The chi-square test needs enough data in every cell. If any expected count, office_test$expected, is below 5, R gives a warning that the result may be inaccurate. Then use fisher.test(), which is exact, or combine small groups.

28.6 Drilling down, and the problem of many tests

Rampur has two dealing officers. Is the high approval rate the office’s, or one officer’s? A natural step is to test every officer against the rest. But with many tests, a new problem appears.

Many tests mean some false alarms. Each test at the 5% level has a 5% chance of a false alarm. Run 20 tests where nothing is unusual, and on average one will come out “significant” anyway. Let us see this with 40 imaginary offices which all approve at exactly the same true rate of 86%.

set.seed(1)
p_values <- map_dbl(1:40, \(i) {
  approved_here <- rbinom(1, size = 300, prob = 0.86)    # this office, 300 claims
  approved_rest <- rbinom(1, size = 12000, prob = 0.86)  # all the others
  prop.test(c(approved_here, approved_rest), c(300, 12000))$p.value
})
sum(p_values < 0.05)
[1] 2

2 of the 40 identical offices look “significantly” different, purely by chance. Reporting them would be unfair to their staff. The remedy is to adjust the p-values for the number of tests. p.adjust() does this. The Holm method is a safe general choice.

sum(p.adjust(p_values, method = "holm") < 0.05)
[1] 0

After adjustment, no false alarms remain. Now the real drill-down. We test each of the 12 officers against all the others, collect the results with broom::tidy(), and adjust.

officer_tests <- map(sort(unique(claims$officer)), \(o) {
  mine <- claims$officer == o
  prop.test(x = c(sum(claims$approved[mine]), sum(claims$approved[!mine])),
            n = c(sum(mine), sum(!mine))) |>
    tidy() |>
    mutate(officer = o, claims = sum(mine), .before = 1)
}) |>
  list_rbind() |>
  mutate(p_adjusted = p.adjust(p.value, method = "holm")) |>
  select(officer, claims, own_rate = estimate1, rest_rate = estimate2, p.value, p_adjusted) |>
  arrange(p.value)

officer_tests |> mutate(across(c(own_rate, rest_rate), \(x) round(x, 3)),
                        across(c(p.value, p_adjusted), \(x) signif(x, 2)))
# A tibble: 12 × 6

The picture is clear. Officer RAM-2 approved 96.3% of claims, and stays significant even after adjustment (adjusted p-value less than 0.0001). The other Rampur officer, RAM-1, approved 86.2%, in line with the department. So the office’s high rate comes from one desk. That is where the audit should look, at a sample of the claims RAM-2 approved, to see whether the checks were actually done.

TipAudit tip

broom::tidy() turns the output of almost any test into a one-row table. Together with map() and list_rbind(), it lets us run the same test over every officer, vendor or district, and get one table to sort, filter and attach to working papers. Always add a column of adjusted p-values when you do this.

28.7 Comparing averages

Rates are about counts. Many audit questions are about amounts and times instead. Is Chandpur slower to settle claims than the other offices? The t-test compares the means of two groups.

claims |>
  summarise(mean_days = round(mean(days_to_settle), 1),
            median_days = median(days_to_settle), .by = office)
# A tibble: 6 × 3
  office   mean_days median_days
  <chr>        <dbl>       <dbl>
1 Devgarh       21.8          20
2 Rampur        21.4          20
3 Belapur       20.3          19
4 Chandpur      27            25
5 Fatehpur      21.3          19
6 Amarpur       21.4          20
chandpur_test <- t.test(days_to_settle ~ (office == "Chandpur"), data = claims)
chandpur_test

    Welch Two Sample t-test

data:  days_to_settle by office == "Chandpur"
t = -9.1182, df = 652.93, p-value < 2.2e-16
alternative hypothesis: true difference in means between group FALSE and group TRUE is not equal to 0
95 percent confidence interval:
 -7.057826 -4.556638
sample estimates:
mean in group FALSE  mean in group TRUE 
           21.22527            27.03250

Chandpur takes 27 days on average, against 21.2 days elsewhere, and the p-value is extremely small. The difference is real, not chance. R uses Welch’s t-test by default, which does not assume that the two groups vary equally. Keep this default.

The formula days_to_settle ~ (office == "Chandpur") reads “days to settle, by whether the office is Chandpur”. The group FALSE (the other offices) comes first in the output.

28.8 When amounts are skewed

The t-test compares means. Money amounts are usually skewed, with many small values and a few very large ones, as we saw in Chapter 25. A handful of large claims can pull a mean up or down, and make the t-test unreliable. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test (also called the Mann-Whitney test) avoids this. It ranks all the values together, and asks whether one group’s values tend to rank higher than the other’s. A single huge claim is then just “the highest rank”, whatever its size.

Are the claims handled by officer DEV-2 larger than everyone else’s?

claims |>
  mutate(group = if_else(officer == "DEV-2", "DEV-2", "Others")) |>
  summarise(claims = n(), mean_amount = round(mean(amount)),
            median_amount = median(amount), .by = group)
# A tibble: 2 × 4
  group  claims mean_amount median_amount
  <chr>   <int>       <dbl>         <dbl>
1 DEV-2     242       36081         26020
2 Others   2758       24549         17980
dev2_test <- wilcox.test(amount ~ (officer == "DEV-2"), data = claims)
dev2_test

    Wilcoxon rank sum test with continuity correction

data:  amount by officer == "DEV-2"
W = 234586, p-value = 1.68e-14
alternative hypothesis: true location shift is not equal to 0

The p-value is less than 0.0001, so DEV-2’s claims are systematically larger. The median tells the same story more plainly than the mean. A follow-up might compare the hospitals, bills and rates in DEV-2’s claims with the others.

Note

Tests like Wilcoxon’s, which work on ranks, are called non-parametric, because they do not assume the data follow a normal distribution. For audit data, which are rarely normal, they are often the safer choice. When both tests are run and they agree, as they usually do on large data, we can be confident. When they disagree, trust the rank test and look for extreme values.

28.9 More than two groups

To compare settlement times across all six offices at once, rather than one office against the rest, use the Kruskal-Wallis test, the rank-based version for several groups.

kruskal.test(days_to_settle ~ office, data = claims)

    Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test

data:  days_to_settle by office
Kruskal-Wallis chi-squared = 86.219, df = 5, p-value < 2.2e-16

It says the offices differ, but not which ones. To find out, compare every pair with pairwise.wilcox.test(), which adjusts the p-values for the many comparisons, just as we learnt above.

pairwise.wilcox.test(claims$days_to_settle, claims$office, p.adjust.method = "holm")

    Pairwise comparisons using Wilcoxon rank sum test with continuity correction 

data:  claims$days_to_settle and claims$office 

         Amarpur Belapur Chandpur Devgarh Fatehpur
Belapur  1.00    -       -        -       -       
Chandpur 4.4e-11 3.0e-15 -        -       -       
Devgarh  1.00    0.26    1.2e-08  -       -       
Fatehpur 1.00    1.00    1.4e-10  1.00    -       
Rampur   1.00    0.76    4.3e-09  1.00    1.00    

P value adjustment method: holm

Every pair involving Chandpur has a tiny p-value, while the other pairs do not differ. (The classic test for comparing the means of several groups is analysis of variance, aov(). It is closely related to linear regression, which we meet in Chapter 37.)

28.10 Does the data follow an expected pattern?

Sometimes we know what pattern the data should follow, and want to test whether it does. This is the chi-square goodness-of-fit test. The number of claims from each office should be roughly in proportion to its staff strength.

staff <- c(Amarpur = 400, Belapur = 360, Chandpur = 340,
           Devgarh = 320, Fatehpur = 300, Rampur = 280)
observed <- table(claims$office)[names(staff)]
rbind(observed = observed, expected = round(sum(observed) * staff / sum(staff)))
         Amarpur Belapur Chandpur Devgarh Fatehpur Rampur
observed     587     527      523     485      445    433
expected     600     540      510     480      450    420
fit_test <- chisq.test(observed, p = staff / sum(staff))
fit_test

    Chi-squared test for given probabilities

data:  observed
X-squared = 1.436, df = 5, p-value = 0.9203

The p-value of 0.92 is large. The claims follow the staff strength well, and there is nothing to report. This, too, is a useful audit result. Not every test should find something, and a clean result lets us move on with confidence.

We have already used goodness-of-fit tests in this book without calling them that. The Benford chi-square in Section 30.5.1 and the digit tests in Chapter 31 compare observed counts of digits with the counts expected under a known pattern.

28.11 Are two things related?

Do larger claims take longer to settle? cor.test() tests whether a correlation is different from zero. For skewed amounts, use Spearman’s rank correlation, method = "spearman".

cor_test <- cor.test(claims$amount, claims$days_to_settle, method = "spearman", exact = FALSE)
cor_test

    Spearman's rank correlation rho

data:  claims$amount and claims$days_to_settle
S = 4.469e+09, p-value = 0.7061
alternative hypothesis: true rho is not equal to 0
sample estimates:
        rho 
0.006888644

The correlation is 0.007, practically zero, and not significant (p = 0.71). Settlement time does not depend on the claim amount here. If it did, say if large claims were settled much faster than small ones, it would be worth asking why.

28.12 Significant is not the same as important

A p-value measures how sure we are that a difference is not chance. It does not measure how large or how important the difference is. Two points follow, and both matter in audit.

With very large data, tiny differences become significant. Two districts, each with two lakh claims, approve 85.0% and 85.4% of them.

prop.test(x = c(170000, 170800), n = c(200000, 200000))$p.value
[1] 0.0003741535

The difference is highly significant, but 0.4 percentage points is unlikely to matter to anyone. Always look at the size of the difference, the effect size, alongside the p-value. For rates, that is the difference in percentage points, and for amounts, the difference in rupees.

With small data, even large differences may not be significant. Two small offices, each with 12 claims, approve 10 and 7 of them, 83% against 58%. With counts this small, prop.test() warns that its approximation may be inaccurate, so we use the exact fisher.test() on the cross-table of approved and rejected claims.

small_offices <- matrix(c(10, 2,    # office 1: approved, rejected
                          7, 5),    # office 2: approved, rejected
                        nrow = 2, byrow = TRUE)
small_test <- fisher.test(small_offices)
small_test$p.value
[1] 0.3707094

A large difference, but far from significant (p = 0.37), because 12 claims are too few to tell. As in Section 28.3, the honest conclusion is “not proven”, not “no difference”.

How much data is enough? The function power.prop.test() answers this before the analysis. How many claims per office do we need to detect a difference between an approval rate of 86% and one of 95%, with an 80% chance of catching it (the power)?

power.prop.test(p1 = 0.86, p2 = 0.95, power = 0.80)

     Two-sample comparison of proportions power calculation 

              n = 165.4353
             p1 = 0.86
             p2 = 0.95
      sig.level = 0.05
          power = 0.8
    alternative = two.sided

NOTE: n is number in *each* group

About 166 claims in each group. For smaller differences, many more are needed. power.t.test() does the same for comparing means.

28.13 Choosing a test

TipAudit tip

Decide the test, and the limit for significance, before looking at the results. If we try test after test until one gives a small p-value, the result means nothing, because with enough attempts chance alone will oblige. Write down the question, the test and the limit in the audit plan, just as we fix the sample size in advance.

28.14 A suggested workflow

  1. State the question in plain words, such as “does this officer approve more claims than the others?”, and the null hypothesis it implies.
  2. Look at the data first. Summarise by group and plot. A test should confirm what you see, not replace looking.
  3. Choose the test from Table 28.2. Prefer rank tests for money amounts, and check that the chi-square test has enough data in each cell.
  4. Fix the significance limit in advance, usually 5%.
  5. When testing many groups, adjust the p-values with p.adjust().
  6. Report the size of the difference with the p-value, in percentage points or rupees.
  7. Treat a significant result as a lead, not a finding. Follow it up by examining the actual records, as with RAM-2’s approved claims.
  8. Treat a non-significant result as “not proven”, especially on small samples, and say what the data could and could not show.
ImportantRemember
  • A test asks one question: is this difference larger than chance would usually produce?
  • The p-value is the chance of seeing a difference at least this large if nothing were unusual. Small means surprising.
  • A false alarm wastes effort and is unfair to the auditee; a miss is audit risk. The significance limit balances the two.
  • Many tests produce false alarms by chance. Adjust the p-values with p.adjust().
  • For skewed amounts, prefer rank-based tests like wilcox.test() and kruskal.test().
  • Significant does not mean important, and not significant does not mean no problem.
