47  Anomaly Detection

Anomalies play a significant role in the field of audit. An anomaly is something that does not fit. It may be a payment far larger than usual, a claim that breaks a pattern, or a month when expenditure suddenly jumps. As auditors examine data, whether financial statements, transactions or other records, such anomalies give valuable leads.

Anomalies often point to problems like these.

  • Fraud. Fictitious transactions, inflated bills, unauthorised access or misappropriation of assets.
  • Errors and misstatements. Wrong entries, keying mistakes and wrong classification.
  • Weak internal controls. Transactions that should have been stopped by a control, but were not.
  • Non-compliance. Payments that break rules, limits or entitlements.
  • Inefficiency. Processes that take unusually long or cost unusually more.

Detecting and investigating anomalies is therefore a core skill for an auditor. In this chapter, we will learn several methods to detect them, from simple boxplots to machine learning methods. Table 47.1 gives a road map.

47.1 Definition and types of anomalies

Anomalies are patterns or data points that deviate significantly from the expected or normal behaviour within a dataset. They are also known as outliers, novelties or deviations. There are three types of anomalies.

  • Point anomalies. A single record which is unusual when compared with the rest of the data. For example, a stationery bill of ₹ 2,00,000 in an office where such bills are usually below ₹ 10,000.
  • Contextual anomalies. A record which is unusual only in a particular context. For example, an electricity bill of ₹ 3 lakh may be normal for an office in May, when air conditioners run all day, but unusual for the same office in December. The amount is not odd by itself. The month makes it odd.
  • Collective anomalies. A group of records which is unusual when seen together, though each record may look normal by itself. For example, twenty cash withdrawals of ₹ 49,000 each from one account within a week. No single withdrawal is unusual. Together, they suggest that someone is avoiding a reporting limit of ₹ 50,000.

47.2 Anomaly detection by inspection

Many times, anomalies or outliers can be spotted just by looking at the data. The summary() function prints the minimum, maximum, lower and upper quartiles, the mean and the median. It gives a sense of how far an extreme point lies from the rest of the data.

As an example, let us take the mean annual temperatures in degrees Fahrenheit in New Haven, Connecticut, from 1912 to 1971. This data is available in base R as nhtemp.

summary(nhtemp)
   Min. 1st Qu.  Median    Mean 3rd Qu.    Max. 
  47.90   50.58   51.20   51.16   51.90   54.60

The easiest way to see how unusual a value is, is a graphical summary like a boxplot. In base R, it is drawn with the boxplot() function. It shows the distribution of values as a box with whiskers, and draws extreme values as separate points. This makes them easy to spot. We can also draw boxplots using ggplot2.

For an audit example, let us take the stationery and printing bills of a district office for one year. Three vendors supply the office. Shree Ram Stationers sends small bills for paper and pens. Kaveri Office Supplies sends somewhat larger bills for toner and files. Modern Printers prints forms and registers, and its bills are the largest. One bill has been planted. It is a bill of Shree Ram Stationers for ₹ 38,000.

Code to create the stationery bills
set.seed(311)
stationery <- tibble(
  vendor = rep(c("Shree Ram Stationers", "Kaveri Office Supplies", "Modern Printers"),
               times = c(40, 30, 20)),
  amount = round(c(rnorm(40, 4500, 1200), rnorm(30, 9500, 2000), rnorm(20, 34000, 7000)), -1)
)
# Planted. A bill far above what this vendor usually charges
stationery$amount[17] <- 38000
glimpse(stationery)
Rows: 90
Columns: 2
$ vendor <chr> "Shree Ram Stationers", "Shree Ram Stationers", "Shree Ram Stat…
$ amount <dbl> 5400, 5300, 3860, 2480, 4530, 6410, 4410, 3570, 5990, 6550, 344…

Figure 47.1 shows both kinds of boxplot. The left panel is the base R boxplot of the nhtemp data, the mean annual temperatures of New Haven. The right panel draws the stationery bills, one box for each vendor.

The planted bill of ₹ 38,000 shows as a red point above the box of Shree Ram Stationers. Notice that the same amount would be quite ordinary for Modern Printers. If we had drawn one box for all the bills together, this bill would have hidden among the printing bills.

The separate points in a boxplot are decided by a simple rule, often called the IQR rule. The box covers the middle 50% of the data, from the lower quartile (Q1) to the upper quartile (Q3). The difference Q3 - Q1 is called the inter-quartile range or IQR. Any value more than 1.5 × IQR below Q1, or above Q3, is drawn as a separate point.

We do not have to read these values from the plot. boxplot.stats() returns them in its out element.

boxplot.stats(nhtemp)$out
[1] 47.9 48.4 54.0 54.6

For the bills, we apply the same rule within each vendor.

stationery |>
  filter(amount %in% boxplot.stats(amount)$out, .by = vendor)
# A tibble: 2 × 2
  vendor               amount
  <chr>                 <dbl>
1 Shree Ram Stationers  38000
2 Modern Printers       19870

The planted bill is listed. So is one unusually small bill of Modern Printers. A value far below the rest is also an outlier. A small printing bill is less likely to hide a loss, but it may point to an order split into parts, so it is worth a glance too.

TipAudit tip

The boxplot is the quickest first check on any amount column. Draw it by group rather than for the whole data. For example, draw a boxplot of bill amounts for each vendor or each head of account. A bill which is normal for the whole office may be very unusual for its own vendor. We saw this idea in the RSF test in Chapter 31.

It is important to note that a point anomaly need not always be an extreme value. A point anomaly can also arise as an unusual combination of values across several columns. We will see such an example in Section 47.10.

A collective anomaly, on the other hand, is a group of records which is unusual together. In Figure 47.2, a period of five consecutive days with high temperatures is shown by red points. These daily temperatures are unusual because they occur together, and are likely caused by the same weather event.

47.3 Grubbs’ test

Visual inspection works well when most data points are grouped together and a few lie clearly apart. But it is a matter of judgement. Statistics offers formal tests to decide whether a value is an outlier. Grubbs’ test, named after Frank Grubbs, is one of these.

Grubbs’ test checks whether the single value lying farthest from the mean could be an outlier. This value will be either the largest or the smallest value in the data. The test assumes that the data is normally distributed. So, before using it, we must check that this assumption is reasonable. A histogram is a quick way to check this. (For other ways of checking normality, refer to the chapter on linear regression.)

Let us run the test on the nhtemp data, which we saw in Figure 47.1. First, the histogram.

hist(nhtemp, breaks = 20)

In Figure 47.3 we can see that the data is roughly bell shaped, so the assumption is nearly satisfied. Let us run the test, using grubbs.test() from the outliers package.

library(outliers)
grubbs.test(nhtemp)

    Grubbs test for one outlier

data:  nhtemp
G = 2.71806, U = 0.87266, p-value = 0.1539
alternative hypothesis: highest value 54.6 is an outlier

How do we read this? The null hypothesis of the test is that there is no outlier. The output shows the alternative hypothesis, that the highest value, 54.6, is an outlier. The p-value is about 0.15, which is more than the usual cut-off of 0.05. So we do not have enough evidence to reject the null hypothesis. In simple words, we cannot conclude that 54.6 is an outlier, though it looked separate in the boxplot.

WarningCaution

Grubbs’ test checks only one value at a time, the most extreme one. If there are several outliers, they can hide each other. For testing several suspected outliers together, a related test called Rosner’s test can be used. It is available as rosnerTest() in the EnvStats package. Also remember that most financial data, like bill amounts, is not normally distributed. It usually has a long tail of large values. For such data, Grubbs’ test is not suitable unless the data is first transformed, for example by taking logs.

47.4 Robust z-scores and peer groups

The best-known way to measure how unusual a value is, is the z-score. It tells how many standard deviations a value lies from the mean. A value with a z-score beyond 3, or below -3, is commonly called an outlier. But the plain z-score has two weaknesses which matter a great deal in audit data.

It is not robust. The mean and the standard deviation are themselves pulled by extreme values. A few large outliers raise the mean and inflate the standard deviation, and so make themselves look less unusual. This is called masking. See this small example.

x <- c(10, 11, 12, 10, 11, 12, 11, 95, 98, 99)
round((x - mean(x)) / sd(x), 2)
 [1] -0.64 -0.62 -0.60 -0.64 -0.62 -0.60 -0.62  1.39  1.46  1.49

The three obviously odd values, 95, 98 and 99, have z-scores below 1.5. They have hidden one another. The remedy is to use measures which outliers cannot pull. These are the median in place of the mean, and the median absolute deviation (MAD) in place of the standard deviation. The MAD is the median of the distances of the values from their median. R’s mad() scales it so that, for normal data, it matches the standard deviation. The result is the robust z-score.

round((x - median(x)) / mad(x), 2)
 [1] -1.01 -0.34  0.34 -1.01 -0.34  0.34 -0.34 56.32 58.34 59.02

Now the three odd values stand out at once. A common rule, suggested by Iglewicz and Hoaglin, is to flag robust z-scores beyond 3.5. For money amounts, which are skewed, compute the score on the logarithm of the amount, as we did in Section 47.11.

It compares everything with everything. The second weakness matters even more. A district hospital spends far more per patient than a primary health centre. Compared with all facilities together, every district hospital looks “unusual”, and a primary health centre spending three times as much as other PHCs looks normal. The fair comparison is with the peer group. That means PHCs with PHCs, and district hospitals with district hospitals.

Let us see both points on the medicine expenditure of 300 health facilities of a State.

Code to create the facility data
set.seed(2028)
types <- c(PHC = 200, CHC = 70, DH = 30)   # primary, community health centres, district hospitals
facilities <- tibble(type = rep(names(types), types)) |>
  mutate(facility = paste0(type, "-", sprintf("%03d", row_number())), .by = type) |>
  mutate(beds = round(case_when(type == "PHC" ~ runif(n(), 6, 10),
                                type == "CHC" ~ runif(n(), 30, 50),
                                TRUE          ~ runif(n(), 100, 300))),
         patients = round(beds * rlnorm(n(), log(140), 0.25)),          # patients per month
         cost_per_patient = round(case_when(type == "PHC" ~ 120, type == "CHC" ~ 330,
                                            TRUE ~ 850) * rlnorm(n(), 0, 0.18)),
         # Planted. Medicine spending far above the facility's own peers
         cost_per_patient = case_when(facility == "PHC-037" ~ 410,
                                      facility == "CHC-012" ~ 1250,
                                      TRUE ~ cost_per_patient),
         medicine_lakh = round(patients * cost_per_patient / 1e5, 2))
facilities |>
  summarise(facilities = n(), median_cost = median(cost_per_patient), .by = type)
# A tibble: 3 × 3
  type  facilities median_cost
  <chr>      <int>       <dbl>
1 PHC          200         118
2 CHC           70         329
3 DH            30         898

First, the plain z-score of the cost per patient, over all 300 facilities.

facilities |>
  mutate(z = (cost_per_patient - mean(cost_per_patient)) / sd(cost_per_patient)) |>
  filter(abs(z) > 3) |>
  count(type)
# A tibble: 2 × 2
  type      n
  <chr> <int>
1 CHC       1
2 DH        7

Of the 8 facilities flagged, 7 are district hospitals, flagged simply for being district hospitals. Now the robust z-score of the log cost, computed within each type with .by.

robust_z <- function(x) (x - median(x)) / mad(x)

facilities <- facilities |>
  mutate(rz_peer = robust_z(log(cost_per_patient)), .by = type)

facilities |>
  filter(abs(rz_peer) > 3.5) |>
  select(facility, type, cost_per_patient, rz_peer) |>
  mutate(rz_peer = round(rz_peer, 1))
# A tibble: 2 × 4
  facility type  cost_per_patient rz_peer
  <chr>    <chr>            <dbl>   <dbl>
1 PHC-037  PHC                410     6.2
2 CHC-012  CHC               1250     7.5

Only the two planted facilities are flagged. PHC-037 spends ₹410 per patient, ordinary for a CHC but more than three times what other PHCs spend, which the plain z-score over all facilities could not see. CHC-012 spends far more than other CHCs.

TipAudit tip

Robust z-scores within peer groups are the simplest anomaly method that an audit team can explain to anyone, and often the most useful. Choose peer groups that should behave alike. Examples are facilities of one type, schools of one level, police stations of one category, DDOs of one department, vendors of one commodity. Use a ratio that removes size, like cost per patient or expenditure per student. And make sure each group has enough members, at least 20 or 30, for the median and MAD to be reliable.

47.5 Unusual combinations, robust Mahalanobis distance

Robust z-scores look at one measure at a time. Some anomalies show only in a combination. When a natural ratio exists, like cost per patient, the ratio captures the combination, and the z-score of the ratio is the best tool. But often the measures are related in a way no single ratio captures.

Take the primary health centres of a district. Larger PHCs see more patients in the outpatient department (OPD) and also conduct more deliveries, so the two numbers rise together. Each delivery in a public facility earns an incentive under the Janani Suraksha Yojana, so inflated delivery figures are a known risk. Now suppose one PHC reports a fairly low OPD but a fairly high number of deliveries. Neither number is unusual by itself. Together, they are.

Code to create the PHC data
set.seed(12)
n_phc <- 200
size  <- rlnorm(n_phc, 0, 0.35)              # some PHCs serve larger populations
phc <- tibble(phc        = sprintf("PHC-%03d", 1:n_phc),
              opd        = round(1100 * size * rlnorm(n_phc, 0, 0.12)),   # patients per month
              deliveries = round(30 * size * rlnorm(n_phc, 0, 0.15)))     # deliveries per month
# Planted. Low OPD (15th percentile) but high deliveries (88th percentile)
phc$opd[77]        <- round(quantile(phc$opd, 0.15))
phc$deliveries[77] <- round(quantile(phc$deliveries, 0.88))
phc <- phc |> mutate(z_opd = robust_z(log(opd)), z_deliveries = robust_z(log(deliveries)))
phc |> filter(phc == "PHC-077") |> mutate(across(starts_with("z_"), \(z) round(z, 2)))
# A tibble: 1 × 5
  phc       opd deliveries z_opd z_deliveries
  <chr>   <dbl>      <dbl> <dbl>        <dbl>
1 PHC-077   745         46  -1.1         1.15

Neither robust z-score of PHC-077 comes anywhere near 3.5. A scatter plot shows the problem at once.

ggplot(phc, aes(opd, deliveries)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.6) +
  geom_point(data = filter(phc, phc == "PHC-077"), colour = "red", size = 3) +
  scale_x_log10() + scale_y_log10() +
  labs(x = "OPD patients per month (log scale)", y = "Deliveries per month (log scale)") +
  theme_minimal()

The Mahalanobis distance measures how far a point lies from the centre of the data, taking account of how the measures vary together. A point along the main band of the scatter, even far out, is not unusual. A point across the band, like PHC-077, is. It works for any number of measures, where scatter plots are no longer possible.

Like the z-score, it needs a centre and a measure of spread, and like the z-score, these are pulled by outliers. So we use robust estimates, from cov.rob() in the package MASS, which comes with R. Its “minimum covariance determinant” method finds the centre and spread of the most tightly packed half of the data, ignoring the rest. We work on the logarithms, since the counts are skewed.

X <- cbind(log(phc$opd), log(phc$deliveries))
robust_fit <- MASS::cov.rob(X, method = "mcd")
phc$mahal <- mahalanobis(X, center = robust_fit$center, cov = robust_fit$cov)

phc |>
  arrange(desc(mahal)) |>
  select(phc, opd, deliveries, mahal) |>
  mutate(mahal = round(mahal, 1)) |>
  head(5)
# A tibble: 5 × 4
  phc       opd deliveries mahal
  <chr>   <dbl>      <dbl> <dbl>
1 PHC-077   745         46  27.6
2 PHC-092   548         23   9.7
3 PHC-001   458         18   9.7
4 PHC-192  1357         23   9.4
5 PHC-029  1413         24   9.3

PHC-077 stands far above the rest. How large is “large”? For normal data, the squared Mahalanobis distance, which is what mahalanobis() returns, follows a chi-square distribution, with as many degrees of freedom as there are measures. So qchisq(0.999, df = 2) gives a cut-off that a normal point exceeds only once in a thousand times.

cutoff <- qchisq(0.999, df = ncol(X))
round(cutoff, 1)
[1] 13.8
phc |> filter(mahal > cutoff) |> pull(phc)
[1] "PHC-077"

Only PHC-077 crosses the cut-off. The next step would be to compare its delivery register with its OPD register, the beds used, and the incentive payments made.

47.6 Seasonal Hybrid ESD algorithm

Grubbs’ test has two limitations. It needs normally distributed data, and it looks only at the extreme values of the whole data. Neither suits a time series with seasonal ups and downs, like monthly sales or quarterly revenue. A value may be normal for one season and unusual for another.

The Seasonal Hybrid ESD (S-H-ESD) algorithm was developed at Twitter for exactly this purpose. It first removes the seasonal pattern, and then looks for unusual values in what remains. It is available in the AnomalyDetection package, which can be installed from GitHub using remotes::install_github("twitter/AnomalyDetection").

Let us take the monthly receipts of stamp duty and registration fees of a sub-registrar office, from April 2018 to March 2025, in ₹ lakh. Receipts rise slowly over the years. They are higher in the winter months and highest in March, when many property deals are closed before the financial year ends. Two months have been planted. In August 2021 the receipts were far above normal. In November 2023 they were far below normal, as if part of the money collected was never brought to account.

Code to create the monthly receipts
set.seed(415)
receipt_months <- seq(as.Date("2018-04-01"), as.Date("2025-03-01"), by = "month")
# Seasonal pattern from April to March. Winter months and March are busier.
receipt_season <- c(0.9, 0.95, 0.9, 0.85, 0.85, 0.9, 1.05, 1.1, 1.0, 1.05, 1.1, 1.35)
fy_position <- ((month(receipt_months) + 8) %% 12) + 1   # April = 1, ..., March = 12

receipts <- tibble(
  month = receipt_months,
  receipts_lakh = round(60 + 0.25 * seq_along(receipt_months) +
                          40 * (receipt_season[fy_position] - 1) +
                          rnorm(length(receipt_months), 0, 2.5), 1)
)
# Planted. A spike in August 2021 and a shortfall in November 2023
spike <- receipts$month == as.Date("2021-08-01")
dip   <- receipts$month == as.Date("2023-11-01")
receipts$receipts_lakh[spike] <- round(receipts$receipts_lakh[spike] * 1.6, 1)
receipts$receipts_lakh[dip]   <- round(receipts$receipts_lakh[dip] * 0.55, 1)
head(receipts)
# A tibble: 6 × 2
  month      receipts_lakh
  <date>             <dbl>
1 2018-04-01          56.4
2 2018-05-01          56.5
3 2018-06-01          65.5
4 2018-07-01          55.3
5 2018-08-01          59  
6 2018-09-01          58

AnomalyDetectionVec() takes the values as a plain vector. Since the data is monthly, the seasonal pattern repeats every 12 records, so we use period = 12.

#remotes::install_github("twitter/AnomalyDetection")
library(AnomalyDetection)
shesd <- AnomalyDetectionVec(receipts$receipts_lakh,
                             period = 12,  # Seasonality
                             plot = TRUE,  # set FALSE when plot not needed
                             direction = 'both' # set 'pos' for higher values
                                                # or 'neg' for lower values
                             )
shesd$anoms
  index anoms
1    41 101.1
2    68  45.4
shesd$plot

In Figure 47.4, the anomalies are marked with blue circles. They are also listed in the $anoms element of the output, with their positions and values. Position 41 is August 2021 and position 68 is November 2023. These are the two planted months. The March peaks are not flagged, because the algorithm has allowed for them as part of the season.

The office would now be asked to explain both months. For the spike, we may check whether some documents of other months were entered in August 2021, or whether one very large deal was registered. For the shortfall, we may compare the receipts booked with the documents registered in November 2023, and with the challans deposited in the treasury.

WarningCaution

S-H-ESD assumes that the seasonal ups and downs stay about the same size over the years. In many financial series they grow along with the level. When that happens, the later peaks look unusual compared with the earlier ones, though they follow the same pattern. This is a common trap with growing financial series. We will see how to deal with it, by working on the log scale, in Section 47.11.

47.7 k-Nearest Neighbour distance score

One big limitation of the methods so far is that they work on a single column, or a single series. In real audit work, data is rarely so simple. A vendor may be described by its total payments, number of bills, average bill and share of round amounts. An anomaly may show up only when all these are seen together. The k-Nearest Neighbour (kNN) method works on many columns at once.

The idea of kNN is that records which are close to each other are similar. For each record, we find its k nearest neighbours, and calculate the average distance to them. A record lying far away from even its nearest neighbours is likely to be an anomaly.

Distance between records is usually measured as the straight-line Euclidean distance. For this to be fair, all columns must be on the same scale. Otherwise a column measured in lakhs will swamp a column measured in single digits. So we must always scale the data first.

Let us take the vendors of a Zila Parishad. For each of its 60 vendors, the accounts section has worked out four indicators for the financial year 2023-24.

  • bills, the number of bills paid.
  • avg_bill_lakh, the average bill, in ₹ lakh.
  • round_pct, the percentage of bills in round amounts, that is, exact multiples of ₹ 1,000.
  • days_to_pay, the median number of days between receipt of a bill and its payment.

The vendors are of three kinds. Suppliers of materials send many small bills. Service providers send fewer, larger bills. Works contractors send a few very large bills. Three vendors have been planted at the end of the data. V-058 is a supplier with unusually large bills, mostly in round amounts, paid within days. V-059 is a supplier which looks ordinary, except that it is paid much faster than other suppliers. V-060 is a service provider whose bills look like those of a works contractor. We will come back to V-060 in Section 47.10.

Code to create the vendor indicators
set.seed(1804)
n_cat  <- c(Supplies = 25, Services = 17, Works = 15)
first  <- c("Shree Ganesh", "Om Sai", "Jai Ambe", "Maa Durga", "Balaji", "Ganga", "Laxmi",
            "Shiv Shakti", "Krishna", "Narmada", "Sri Venkateswara", "Kaveri", "Annapurna",
            "Bharat", "Gayatri", "Hanuman", "Mahalaxmi", "Saraswati", "Vishwakarma", "Umiya")

47.8 Local Outlier Factor

kNN is good at finding records which are far from all others. These are sometimes called global anomalies. But it can miss records which lie close to a group, yet are not like their immediate neighbours. These are called local anomalies.

To understand the difference, see the dummy data in Figure 47.7. Think of it as sales and profit of branches of a company.

The red points are global outliers. They are far from everything else. The blue points are not far from the tight group on the right. But that group is very tightly packed, and the blue points do not share its tightness. So they are local anomalies. kNN may miss them, because their distance from the group is small in absolute terms.

The Local Outlier Factor (LOF) solves this. Instead of distance, it looks at density, that is, how crowded the neighbourhood of a record is. It compares the density around each record with the density around its neighbours.

  • A score close to 1 means the record is as crowded as its neighbours. It is normal.
  • A score clearly above 1 means the record is in a much emptier area than its neighbours. It may be an anomaly.

We will use the lof() function from the dbscan package. Its second argument is the number of neighbours to consider, like k in kNN. Let us apply it to the same vendor data, using all four indicators.

47.9 Isolation Forest

Isolation Forest takes a completely different approach. It does not measure distance or density. Instead, it asks a simple question. How easy is it to separate this record from all the others?

Imagine we pick a column at random, and a random cut-point within its range. This splits the data into two parts. We repeat this on each part, again and again, until every record sits alone. Such a series of random splits is called an isolation tree. A normal record, sitting in a crowd, needs many splits before it is alone. An anomaly, sitting far from the crowd, gets separated in very few splits. So the number of splits (the path length in the tree) tells us how unusual a record is.

One tree is based on random choices, so its result can vary. A forest is a collection of many such trees. Averaging over many trees gives a stable score.

We will use the isotree package, which is available on CRAN. The main function is isolation.forest(). Its argument ntrees sets the number of trees. sample_size sets how many records are drawn to build each tree. We also set ndim = 1, which gives the classic isolation forest that splits on one column at a time, and a seed so that the results can be reproduced.

An older package, isofor, is sometimes mentioned in tutorials, but it is available only on GitHub and is no longer maintained. isotree does the same job, is faster, and is regularly updated on CRAN. It can be installed with install.packages("isotree").

47.10.1 How does Gower distance work?

kNN and LOF need a distance between every pair of records. For numbers this is easy. But what is the distance between “Air” and “Sleeper”, or between “Group A” and “Group C”? Gower distance answers this in a simple way.

  • For a numeric column, it takes the difference between the two values and divides it by the range (maximum minus minimum) of that column. So the result is always between 0 and 1.
  • For a categorical column, the distance is 0 if the two values are the same, and 1 if they are different.
  • The final distance is the average of these column-wise distances.

So every column contributes equally, and the final distance is always between 0 and 1. This is why we did not have to scale the data before using daisy().

47.11.1 How does this method work?

tk_anomaly_diagnostics() works in two steps.

  1. It first splits the series into three parts, using a method called STL decomposition. The trend is the long-term rise or fall. The season is the pattern which repeats every year, or every week. The remainder is whatever is left after removing the trend and season.
  2. It then looks for unusually large values in the remainder only. The usual rule of the boxplot is used, so a value far outside the middle 50% of the remainders is called an anomaly.

So a value is called an anomaly only if it cannot be explained by the trend and the usual seasonal pattern. We learnt about these components of a time series in Chapter 46.

47.12 Audit use case, an odd month of expenditure

Every auditor in government knows the March rush. Offices spend much more in March than in other months, to use up the budget before the financial year ends. So a high expenditure in March is expected. What an auditor wants to find is a month which is unusual even after allowing for this pattern.

Let us create six years of monthly expenditure of an office, from April 2019 to March 2025, in ₹ lakh. The expenditure grows a little every year, and March is always much higher than other months. We have planted one odd month, October 2023, when some unusual payments were made.

47.13 When did behaviour change? Change points

The time series methods of Section 47.11 find single periods that are unusual. Sometimes the question is different. When did a series shift to a new level, and stay there? A vendor’s rate that steps up when a new officer takes charge, a sudden and lasting rise in a hospital’s claims after it was empanelled with a new third-party administrator, or collections that drop after a change in the inspection system. Such a lasting shift is not one odd month, so methods looking for odd months may miss it. Change point detection looks for exactly these shifts.

Let us take the rate charged per bag of cement by one supplier to a public works division, month by month for four years.

Code to create the cement rates
set.seed(7)
cement <- tibble(month = seq(as.Date("2021-04-01"), by = "month", length.out = 48),
                 # Planted. A lasting step up after the 30th month, and a one-off spike in month 12
                 rate  = round(c(rnorm(30, 365, 8), rnorm(18, 405, 8))))
cement$rate[12] <- 440

The package changepoint finds the points where the mean of a series changes, with cpt.mean(). The PELT method searches efficiently for any number of change points. One preparation is needed. The function measures changes in units of the series’ natural month-to-month noise, and assumes that noise has a standard deviation of 1. So we first divide the series by a robust estimate of its noise, the MAD of the month-to-month differences divided by \(\sqrt{2}\).

library(changepoint)
noise <- mad(diff(cement$rate)) / sqrt(2)
cp <- cpt.mean(cement$rate / noise, method = "PELT")
cpts(cp)
[1] 11 12 30

cpts() gives the positions after which the mean changes. They are 11, 12, 30. Let us read them with the dates. The changes after months 11 and 12 enclose a single month, March 2022. That is the one-off spike, a point anomaly, which the method sees as a tiny segment of its own. The change after month 30, September 2023, is the lasting shift. The average rate in each segment shows its size.

TipAudit tip

Then compare the dates with known events, such as rate revisions, transfers of officers and new contracts. A shift with no explanation is a strong lead. cpt.meanvar() looks for changes in both the mean and the variability, which can reveal, for example, a period when rates suddenly stopped varying, as if copied.

47.14 Combining several methods

Each method in this chapter sees anomalies in its own way. A robust z-score sees one extreme measure. The Mahalanobis distance sees a broken relationship. An isolation forest sees records that are easy to separate from the rest, and LOF sees records unlike their own neighbours. A record flagged by several methods is a stronger lead than one flagged by a single method, and different methods catch different kinds of irregularity. So in practice we run several, and combine their verdicts into one ranked list for examination.

The scores of different methods are on different scales. A z-score may be 4, a Mahalanobis distance 25 and an isolation score 0.6. They cannot be added directly. The simplest fair way is to convert each method’s scores into ranks, or rather percentile ranks between 0 and 1, and average them. A record which every method places near the top gets a combined score near 1.

Let us add a third measure to our PHC data, medicine expenditure, and plant two more cases of different kinds.

Code to add medicine expenditure and two more planted cases
set.seed(21)
phc <- phc |>
  mutate(medicine_lakh = round(opd * 120 * rlnorm(n(), 0, 0.15) / 1e5, 2))
# Planted. PHC-140 spends far more on medicines than its OPD explains

# Planted. PHC-021 reports far less of everything than any other PHC
phc$opd[21]           <- round(min(phc$opd) * 0.45)
phc$deliveries[21]    <- max(1, round(min(phc$deliveries) * 0.45))
phc$medicine_lakh[21] <- round(min(phc$medicine_lakh) * 0.45, 2)

Now we score every PHC with four methods. All of them work on the logarithms of the three measures.

library(isotree)
library(dbscan)

logs <- phc |> transmute(l_opd = log(opd), l_del = log(deliveries), l_med = log(medicine_lakh))

scores <- phc |>
  transmute(
    phc,
    # 1. largest robust z-score among the three measures, and medicine per OPD patient
    robust_z = pmax(abs(robust_z(logs$l_opd)), abs(robust_z(logs$l_del)),
                    abs(robust_z(logs$l_med)), abs(robust_z(logs$l_med - logs$l_opd))),
    # 2. robust Mahalanobis distance
    mahal = {
      fit <- MASS::cov.rob(as.matrix(logs), method = "mcd")
      mahalanobis(as.matrix(logs), fit$center, fit$cov)
    },
    # 3. isolation forest score
    iforest = {
      set.seed(1)
      predict(isolation.forest(logs, ntrees = 200), logs)
    },
    # 4. local outlier factor, on scaled values
    lof = lof(scale(logs), minPts = 10)
  )

Each column is a score where higher means more unusual. Now we turn each into a percentile rank with percent_rank(), and average.

TipAudit tip

Keep the individual method scores alongside the combined score in your working papers. When a record is examined, they explain why it was selected. For example, “medicine spending three times what its OPD explains” (robust z of the ratio), or “deliveries out of line with OPD” (Mahalanobis). An explained selection is much easier to defend than a single number.

Note

Averaging and voting both give every method an equal say. If experience shows that one method finds more real issues in your data, give it a larger weight. And when outcomes of past examinations are available, consider the supervised methods of Chapter 39 and Chapter 41, which learn directly from what was found.

47.15 Choosing a method

With so many methods, which one should an auditor use? Table 47.3 gives a simple guide.

47.16 A suggested workflow

  1. Understand the data. Know what each column means, and what “normal” looks like for the business.
  2. Apply the known rules first. Limits, entitlements and rates can be checked directly, without any algorithm.
  3. Look at the data. Draw boxplots and histograms, group-wise. Many anomalies are visible at this stage itself.
  4. Prepare the data for the algorithm. Scale numeric columns for kNN and LOF. Use the log for money amounts. Convert categorical columns to factors.
  5. Compare like with like. Use peer groups and size-free ratios, so that big entities are not flagged merely for being big.
  6. Run more than one method, and combine them by rank. A record flagged by several methods is a stronger lead.
  7. Rank, do not label. Sort records by score and examine them from the top.
  8. Verify with documents. Explain every flagged record from the records, before treating it as a finding.
ImportantRemember

An anomaly is only unusual. It is not necessarily wrong. Many anomalies have perfectly good explanations, like a one-time bulk purchase or a new scheme. Anomaly detection tells the auditor where to look. Only the examination of records can tell what happened.
