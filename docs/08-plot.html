21  Charts in Base R

Suppose a Public Works Division gives us its bill register for the year. It has three thousand bills. Officers of the division may approve a bill up to ₹1 lakh. Anything above needs sanction from a higher authority. We want to know whether some bills were kept just below that limit on purpose. We also want to know whether spending rushed up in March, and whether one office pays much larger bills than the others.

A table of three thousand rows will not answer these questions. Totals and averages will not answer them either. A chart will. A histogram of the bill amounts shows a pile of bills just under ₹1 lakh at a glance. A line chart of monthly spending shows the March rush. A box plot puts the offices side by side. This is why we look at the data before we summarise it.

Charts also help us tell others what we found. A well drawn chart in an audit report says more than a table of numbers. It also helps us spot errors and odd values in the data.

In this chapter we learn the charts that come with base R, without any extra package. We will use plot(), barplot(), hist(), boxplot() and density plots, and arrange several charts on one page with par(). All the examples use audit data. At the end we use a histogram to find bills split to stay below the limit.

Note

In Chapter 22 we will learn the ggplot2 package. It is very flexible, and is built on a theory known as the “grammar of graphics”. This grammar was created by Leland Wilkinson. It is also used by other software, such as Tableau1 and Plotly. Base R charts are still worth learning. They need no package and are very quick for a first look at the data.

21.1 Our data, a bill register and a scheme

We will use two small datasets in this chapter.

The first, bills, is the bill register of a Public Works Division for the financial year 2024-25. The division has five sub-divisional offices. Each bill has a number, a date, the office, the head of expenditure and the amount in rupees. We have planted two irregularities in it. Our charts should bring both out.

The second, blocks, gives the number of beneficiaries and the expenditure of a state scheme in 40 blocks of a district. The scheme pays about ₹6,000 a year to each beneficiary. So the expenditure of a block should rise in step with its beneficiaries. One block does not follow this rule.

Code to create the bill register and the block data
set.seed(77)
offices <- c("Nandgaon", "Sonpur", "Bhimtal", "Kesarganj", "Rajnagar")
heads   <- c("Repairs", "Supplies", "Electricity", "Travel", "Office expenses")
fy_days <- seq(as.Date("2024-04-01"), as.Date("2025-03-31"), by = "day")

# Planted. Days in March are three times as likely to get a bill (a March rush)
day_wt <- ifelse(format(fy_days, "%m") == "03", 3, 1)

n <- 3000
bills <- data.frame(
  office    = sample(offices, n, TRUE),
  head      = sample(heads, n, TRUE, prob = c(3, 3, 2, 1, 2)),
  bill_date = sample(fy_days, n, TRUE, prob = day_wt),
  amount    = round(rlnorm(n, log(30000), 0.9))
)

# Planted. 60 repair bills of Kesarganj kept just below the limit of Rs 1 lakh
split_bills <- data.frame(
  office    = "Kesarganj",
  head      = "Repairs",
  bill_date = sample(fy_days, 60, TRUE),
  amount    = round(runif(60, 94000, 99999))
)

bills <- rbind(bills, split_bills)
bills <- bills[order(bills$bill_date), ]
rownames(bills) <- NULL
bills$bill_no <- sprintf("BL/24-25/%04d", seq_len(nrow(bills)))
fy_months <- month.abb[c(4:12, 1:3)]
bills$month <- factor(month.abb[as.integer(format(bills$bill_date, "%m"))],
                      levels = fy_months)
bills$quarter <- paste0("Q", (as.integer(bills$month) - 1) %/% 3 + 1)
bills <- bills[, c("bill_no", "bill_date", "month", "quarter", "office", "head", "amount")]

# Scheme data of 40 blocks, at about Rs 6,000 per beneficiary
blocks <- data.frame(
  block         = sprintf("Block %02d", 1:40),
  beneficiaries = round(runif(40, 800, 5000))
)
blocks$expenditure_lakh <- round(blocks$beneficiaries * 6000 * runif(40, 0.92, 1.08) / 1e5, 1)
# Planted. Block 27 spends far more than its beneficiaries justify
blocks$expenditure_lakh[27] <- round(blocks$expenditure_lakh[27] * 1.7, 1)

Let us look at the first few rows of each.

2 BL/24-25/0002 2024-04-01   Apr      Q1    Sonpur        Supplies  21666

6 BL/24-25/0006 2024-04-02   Apr      Q1  Rajnagar          Travel  27177
head(blocks)
     block beneficiaries expenditure_lakh
1 Block 01          4798            288.2
2 Block 02          3440            192.6
3 Block 03          2628            169.2
4 Block 04          1233             76.9
5 Block 05          3879            228.7
6 Block 06          1547             90.7

The register has 3,060 bills worth ₹14.3 crore in all.

21.2 Using plot()

The plot() function is very versatile. Its main use is to draw scatter plots. It can also draw line charts, box plots and other charts, depending on the data we give it. Its basic syntax is:

plot(x, y, ...)

The x and y arguments are the values for the x-axis and the y-axis. The ... can hold many optional arguments that change how the chart looks. The most common are listed here.

  • xlab is the label of the x-axis.
  • ylab is the label of the y-axis.
  • main is the main title of the chart.
  • xlim and ylim set the limits of the x-axis and the y-axis.
  • type is the type of chart. "p" draws points, "l" draws lines and "b" draws both.
  • col is the colour of the points or lines.
  • pch is the symbol used for the points.
  • cex is the size of the points and text.
Note

Here x and y are vectors, such as blocks$beneficiaries. In Chapter 22 we will give column names instead. The examples below make this clear.

21.2.1 A scatter plot

Let us plot the expenditure of each block against its number of beneficiaries.

plot(blocks$beneficiaries,
     blocks$expenditure_lakh,
     xlab = "Number of beneficiaries",
     ylab = "Expenditure (Rs lakh)",
     main = "Expenditure and beneficiaries of 40 blocks",
     pch = 19)

The points lie close to a straight line, as they should. One point sits well above the line. It is Block 27. It spent ₹9,858 per beneficiary, against a median of ₹6,086 for all blocks.

We can mark that point in red with points(), and name it with text(). Both add to a chart that is already drawn.

plot(blocks$beneficiaries, blocks$expenditure_lakh,
     xlab = "Number of beneficiaries", ylab = "Expenditure (Rs lakh)",
     pch = 19, col = "grey50")
points(blocks$beneficiaries[odd], blocks$expenditure_lakh[odd],
       pch = 19, col = "red", cex = 1.5)
text(blocks$beneficiaries[odd], blocks$expenditure_lakh[odd],
     labels = blocks$block[odd], pos = 2)

A scatter plot shows that two columns move together. It cannot prove that one causes the other. Here, though, the rule of the scheme tells us they should move together. So a point far from the line is a lead to follow up.

21.2.2 A line chart

Let us total the bills of each month with tapply(), in lakh. Then we draw the totals as a line.

monthly <- tapply(bills$amount, bills$month, sum) / 1e5
round(monthly, 1)
  Apr   May   Jun   Jul   Aug   Sep   Oct   Nov   Dec   Jan   Feb   Mar 
 96.3 102.4  98.0  93.1 102.7  97.4 108.4 111.4 127.5 113.2  77.7 302.3 
plot(1:12, monthly,
     type = "b",
     xlab = "Month of the financial year (1 = April)",
     ylab = "Amount (Rs lakh)",
     main = "Monthly total of bills, 2024-25")

Here type = "b" draws both points and lines. With type = "l" we would get lines only. The total for March is ₹302.3 lakh. That is about 3 times the median of the other months. This is the March rush we planted.

21.2.3 A bar chart of counts

table() counts the bills under each head. barplot() draws the counts as bars.

barplot(table(bills$head),
        xlab = "Head of expenditure",
        ylab = "Number of bills",
        main = "Bills by head of expenditure")

21.2.4 A box plot

When x is a factor and y is numeric, plot() draws one box plot for each level of the factor. Let us compare the bill amounts under each head.

plot(x = factor(bills$head),
     y = bills$amount,
     log = "y",
     main = "Bill amounts by head",
     xlab = "Head of expenditure",
     ylab = "Amount (Rs, log scale)",
     col = "darkgray")

Bill amounts are skewed. Most bills are small and a few are very large. On an ordinary scale the boxes would be squashed at the bottom. So we used log = "y". On a log scale, each step up the axis multiplies the amount, rather than adding to it. The boxes now have room to show their spread.

21.2.5 A density plot

A density plot is a smooth version of a histogram. We make it with density(), and draw it with plot().

plot(density(bills$amount / 1000),
     main = "Density of bill amounts",
     xlab = "Amount (Rs thousand)", ylab = "Density", col = "blue")

The curve rises sharply near zero and has a long tail to the right. That is the usual shape of amounts in accounts.

Let us now learn the other chart functions of base R.

21.3 Bar plots using barplot()

As the name suggests, barplot() draws bar charts. Its basic syntax is barplot(height, ...). Here height is a vector giving the height of each bar. The other arguments can be seen with ?barplot.

21.3.1 A basic bar chart

We already have the monthly totals in monthly. The argument names.arg gives the label under each bar.

barplot(monthly,
        names.arg = levels(bills$month),
        main = "Monthly total of bills, 2024-25",
        xlab = "Month", ylab = "Amount (Rs lakh)",
        las = 2)

The argument las = 2 turns the labels on the x-axis so that they all fit.

21.3.2 A bar chart of summarised data

Let us find the average bill of each office with tapply(), and draw the averages as horizontal bars.

office_mean <- tapply(bills$amount, bills$office, mean) / 1000
round(office_mean, 1)
  Bhimtal Kesarganj  Nandgaon  Rajnagar    Sonpur 
     47.5      48.4      45.0      46.1      46.7 
old_par <- par(mar = c(5, 7, 4, 2))   # wider left margin for the names
barplot(height = office_mean,
        main = "Average bill by office",
        xlab = "Average bill (Rs thousand)",
        col = "steelblue",
        horiz = TRUE,
        las = 1,
        cex.names = 0.8)
par(old_par)

horiz = TRUE draws the bars sideways. las = 1 keeps the office names upright, and cex.names = 0.8 makes them a little smaller. The office names need more room on the left than R leaves by default. So we widened the left margin with par(mar = ), and restored it afterwards. We learn more about par() later in this chapter.

The averages are close. Kesarganj is a little ahead, at ₹48.4 thousand. An average alone does not show why. We will find out at the end of this chapter.

21.3.3 A grouped bar chart

Next, let us total the bills by head and by quarter. When tapply() gets two grouping columns in a list(), it gives a matrix. Each row is a head and each column a quarter.

head_qtr <- tapply(bills$amount, list(bills$head, bills$quarter), sum) / 1e5
round(head_qtr, 1)
                  Q1   Q2   Q3    Q4
Electricity     47.1 51.9 62.6  73.1
Office expenses 58.1 49.6 64.2  93.0
Repairs         92.4 82.7 99.9 144.3
Supplies        74.6 81.3 89.7 142.9
Travel          24.5 27.6 30.8  39.9
barplot(height = head_qtr,
        main = "Spending by head and quarter",
        xlab = "Quarter", ylab = "Amount (Rs lakh)",
        col = c("red", "green", "blue", "yellow", "purple"),
        beside = TRUE,
        legend.text = rownames(head_qtr),
        args.legend = list(x = "topleft", cex = 0.8))

barplot() draws one group of bars for each column of the matrix. Inside a group, it draws one bar, in its own colour, for each row. So we get a group for each quarter, with one bar per head. The legend names the rows. Every head rises in the fourth quarter, because of the March rush.

21.3.4 A stacked bar chart

The same matrix with beside = FALSE stacks the rows inside each column. Now each bar is a quarter, and each coloured piece is a head.

barplot(head_qtr,
        main = "Spending by quarter and head",
        xlab = "Quarter", ylab = "Amount (Rs lakh)",
        col = c("red", "green", "blue", "yellow", "purple"),
        legend.text = rownames(head_qtr),
        args.legend = list(x = "topleft", cex = 0.8),
        beside = FALSE)

A stacked bar shows the total of each quarter, and how the heads make it up. A grouped bar is better for comparing one head across quarters.

21.3.5 A bar chart from a formula

We can also give barplot() a formula and a data frame. aggregate() makes the data frame of totals. Then amount ~ office means “amount for each office”.

office_total <- aggregate(amount ~ office, data = bills, FUN = sum)
office_total$amount <- office_total$amount / 1e5
barplot(amount ~ office, data = office_total,
        ylab = "Amount (Rs lakh)")

21.4 Histograms using hist()

The hist() function draws a histogram. A histogram shows how the values of a numeric column are spread. The basic syntax is:

hist(x, breaks = "Sturges", freq = TRUE, main = NULL,
     xlab = NULL, ylab = "Frequency", ...)

The arguments are these.

  • x is the numeric vector to be plotted.
  • breaks sets the bins. By default R chooses the number of bins by the Sturges formula. We can give a different number of bins, or a vector of break points.
  • freq chooses what the y-axis shows. If TRUE, it shows the number of values in each bin. If FALSE, it shows the density.
  • main is the title of the histogram.
  • xlab and ylab are the labels of the axes.
  • ... holds more arguments for the chart, such as col, border and xlim.

Let us draw a histogram of the bill amounts, in thousands of rupees.

hist(bills$amount / 1000, breaks = 50, col = "lightblue",
     xlab = "Amount (Rs thousand)", ylab = "Number of bills",
     main = "Histogram of bill amounts")

Most bills are below ₹50 thousand. A few run to several lakh. A long tail on the right like this is called right skew.

Skewed amounts are easier to see on a log scale. log10() turns ₹10,000 into 4, ₹1 lakh into 5 and ₹10 lakh into 6. Let us draw the histogram of log10(amount) as a density, with freq = FALSE. Then we add a density curve on top of it with lines().

hist(log10(bills$amount),
     freq = FALSE,
     breaks = 40,
     col = "grey85",
     xlab = "log10 of amount (4 = Rs 10,000, 5 = Rs 1 lakh)",
     main = "Histogram, with density curve")
# add density curve
lines(density(log10(bills$amount)), col = "blue", lwd = 2)

lines() adds a layer to the chart already drawn. The bars and the curve now have a smooth, bell-like shape. Look carefully at the bar just to the left of 5, that is, just below ₹1 lakh. It stands a little above the curve, while its neighbour on the right falls below it. The sign is faint here, among three thousand bills. We will come back to it.

21.5 Box plots using boxplot()

A box plot is also called a box and whiskers plot. It shows the spread of a numeric column through a few summary values. Figure 21.14 shows how to read one.

The syntax is boxplot(x, ...). Here x is a numeric vector, or a list of numeric vectors. The ... holds more arguments to change how the chart looks.

boxplot(bills$amount / 1000, ylab = "Amount (Rs thousand)")

The points above the top whisker are values far from the middle of the data. Here there are 197 of them. That is about 6% of all bills. With skewed amounts many bills fall beyond the whisker, and most of them are genuine large bills. So a point beyond the whisker is a candidate to look at, not an error.

Box plots are most useful when drawn side by side for several groups. We can give a formula, such as amount ~ office, as we did for barplot(). Let us compare the offices.

boxplot(amount ~ office,
        data = bills,
        log = "y",
        col = "lightgray",
        xlab = "Office", ylab = "Amount (Rs, log scale)")

The thick line in each box is the median. Kesarganj has the highest median bill, ₹34,160, against ₹28,663 to ₹31,274 for the other offices. The difference is not large. A box plot shows the overall spread well, but it can hide a narrow pile of values. We need the histogram for that.

WarningCaution

A log scale cannot show zero or negative values. With log = "y", R leaves such values out of some charts with a warning. Other charts, like boxplot(), stop with an error. Real registers often have zero amounts (cancelled bills) or negative ones (reversals). Count and examine them separately before drawing a chart on a log scale.

21.7 Use of par()

One useful function of base R graphics is par(). It sets or reads the graphical parameters. In the following example, its argument mfrow = c(nr, nc) draws the next charts in a grid of nr rows and nc columns on the same page.

Here we use a famous built-in dataset, Anscombe’s quartet. It has four small datasets, created by the statistician Francis Anscombe in 1973 to show why we must look at data. Let us first look at their summary statistics.

data.frame(set  = 1:4,
           mean_x = sapply(1:4, \(i) mean(anscombe[[paste0("x", i)]])),
           mean_y = sapply(1:4, \(i) round(mean(anscombe[[paste0("y", i)]]), 2)),
           cor_xy = sapply(1:4, \(i) round(cor(anscombe[[paste0("x", i)]],
                                              anscombe[[paste0("y", i)]]), 3)))
  set mean_x mean_y cor_xy
1   1      9    7.5  0.816
2   2      9    7.5  0.816
3   3      9    7.5  0.816
4   4      9    7.5  0.817

The four datasets have the same means and practically the same correlation. Now let us draw them.

old_par <- par(mfrow = c(2, 2))
purrr::walk(1:4,
     ~plot(anscombe[[paste0('x', .x)]],
           anscombe[[paste0('y', .x)]],
           xlab = paste0('x', .x),
           ylab = paste0('y', .x),
           main = paste("Anscombe's Quartet Chart No.", .x))
     )
par(old_par)   # restore the earlier settings

The pictures are completely different. Only the first is a normal straight line relationship. The second is a curve. The third is a straight line spoiled by one outlier. In the fourth, a single point creates the whole correlation. Summary statistics alone would never tell us this. This is the strongest argument for always looking at the data.

par() returns the old settings. We saved them in old_par and restored them at the end. Otherwise all later charts would also be drawn four to a page.

The same idea is handy in audit. We can draw a histogram, a box plot, a monthly line and a bar chart of a new register on one page, and take in the whole picture at once.

old_par <- par(mfrow = c(2, 2))
hist(log10(bills$amount), breaks = 40, main = "Amounts (log10)", xlab = NULL)
boxplot(amount ~ quarter, data = bills, log = "y", main = "By quarter",
        xlab = NULL, ylab = NULL)
plot(1:12, monthly, type = "b", main = "Monthly total (Rs lakh)",
     xlab = "Month (1 = April)", ylab = NULL)
barplot(table(bills$office), main = "Bills by office", las = 2, cex.names = 0.7)
par(old_par)

21.8 Audit use case, payments just below a limit

Officers of our division may approve a bill up to ₹1 lakh. Anything above needs a higher authority, or a tender. A common irregularity is to split a larger purchase into several bills, each just below the limit. A histogram of bill amounts can show this at a glance. Let us draw the bills between ₹50,000 and ₹1.5 lakh, in bins of ₹2,500.

near_limit <- bills$amount[bills$amount >= 50000 & bills$amount <= 150000]
hist(near_limit,
     breaks = seq(50000, 150000, by = 2500),
     col = "grey80", border = "white",
     xlab = "Amount of bill (Rs)", main = NULL)
abline(v = 100000, col = "red", lwd = 2)

The number of bills falls steadily as amounts rise, as expected. The exception is a sharp spike just below ₹1 lakh, and a drop just after it. The bins of ₹2,500 just under the limit hold far more bills than their neighbours. Such a spike is a strong lead for splitting of purchases.

The next step is to see whose bills these are. Let us take the bills from ₹95,000 up to the limit and count them by office and head.

just_below <- bills[bills$amount >= 95000 & bills$amount < 100000, ]
table(just_below$office, just_below$head)
           
            Electricity Office expenses Repairs Supplies Travel
  Bhimtal             2               2       2        0      0
  Kesarganj           1               3      54        3      0
  Nandgaon            1               0       0        2      0
  Rajnagar            1               0       0        2      1
  Sonpur              3               1       2        1      0

Of the 81 bills in this narrow range, 54 are repair bills of Kesarganj. The other offices have only a few each, spread over the heads. Let us draw them as bars to show the point in a report.

barplot(sort(table(just_below$office), decreasing = TRUE),
        col = c("firebrick", rep("grey70", 4)),
        ylab = "Number of bills", main = "Bills just below Rs 1 lakh")

The auditor would now list the 54 repair bills of Kesarganj, sorted by date. Bills for the same work, to the same contractor, on nearby dates point to one job split into many. Ask the office for the estimates and the work orders. Then check who approved each bill and whether the combined value needed higher sanction. Put the chart, the list and the replies in the working papers. We test this pattern more formally in Chapter 31.

TipAudit tip

Choose the bins carefully. Too wide a bin hides the spike. Here we used bins of ₹2,500 and limited the chart to the range around the limit. Use abline() to mark the limit, so that the reader sees the point at once.

WarningCaution

By default, hist() counts a value that falls exactly on a break in the bin to its left. So a bill of exactly ₹1 lakh is counted in the bin just below the limit. Keep this in mind when the rule says “up to ₹1 lakh”, and check the bills exactly at the limit separately.

21.9 A suggested workflow

  1. Look before you summarise. Draw a quick chart of any new data, like a bill register, before you work out totals. Anscombe’s quartet showed that summary statistics alone can hide very different patterns.
  2. Check the spread of amounts with hist(). A histogram of amounts shows skew, outliers and unusual spikes. Try a few values of breaks, since bins that are too wide hide the pattern. Use log10() for skewed amounts.
  3. Mark the limits with abline(). Draw the financial limit, say ₹1 lakh, on the chart. A spike just below the line is a lead for split purchases.
  4. Compare groups with boxplot(). Use a formula like amount ~ office to compare the spread across offices or heads. Points beyond the whiskers are candidates to look at, not errors.
  5. Count and total categories with barplot(). Count bills with table(), or total them with tapply(), and draw them as bars. Use beside = TRUE to compare groups side by side, or stacked bars to show the make-up of each total.
  6. See trends with a line chart. Plot the monthly totals with type = "b". A rush in March stands out at once.
  7. See relationships with plot(). A scatter plot of two columns shows whether they move together. Mark the odd points with points() and text(). It shows association, not cause.
  8. Put related charts together with par(). Use par(mfrow = ) to see several charts on one page. Restore the old settings afterwards.
  9. Follow up the leads. Take the bills in the unusual bin or group, and examine them by office, date and supporting documents.
ImportantRemember

A chart is the quickest way to see what the numbers hide. Look at the data before trusting any summary. But a spike or an outlier on a chart is only a lead. Confirm it from the original records before reporting.
