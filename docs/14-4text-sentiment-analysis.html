49  Sentiment Analysis

When we read a comment like “Doctors were very good and caring”, we know at once that the writer is happy. “Staff was rude and the ward was dirty” tells us the opposite. Sentiment analysis tries to make a computer do the same. It measures whether a piece of text expresses a positive or a negative feeling, and how strongly.

For an auditor, sentiment is a quick way to sense where things may be going wrong. Thousands of feedback comments, survey responses or grievances cannot all be read. But if the comments about one hospital, one office or one scheme are much more negative than about others, that is where to look first.

49.1 Where can sentiment analysis help an auditor?

  • Beneficiary feedback on schemes, hospitals, schools or ration shops. Units with unusually negative feedback deserve attention.
  • Grievances, to find which ones express the strongest distress, so that serious cases are not lost in the volume.
  • Survey responses collected during performance audits, where hundreds of beneficiaries answer open questions.
  • Trends over time. A sudden fall in sentiment about a service may show the effect of a policy change or a breakdown.
  • Testing what people say against what records show. A hospital whose records look perfect, but whose patients are unhappy, raises questions.

49.2 How does it work?

The simplest and most transparent method uses a sentiment dictionary, also called a lexicon. It is a list of words, each marked as positive or negative. To score a comment, we break it into words, look up each word in the dictionary, and count the positive and negative words. The difference gives a sentiment score.

This is called the dictionary-based or lexicon-based approach. It is easy to understand and explain, which matters in audit. Its weaknesses, which we will see shortly, are also easy to understand and partly fix.

49.6 Scoring each comment

We follow the steps of the last chapter. Break the comments into words, and then keep only the words found in the dictionary. inner_join() does this. It keeps only rows that have a match in both tables.

feedback_words <- feedback |>

  inner_join(bing, by = "word")

Now we count the positive and negative words in each comment. The score of a comment is the number of positive words minus the number of negative words.

scores <- feedback_words |>

49.6.1 Does the score agree with the stars?

Let us check how well the score agrees with the rating given by the patient. The correlation, which we met in the PCA chapter, is a single number for this.

cor(feedback_scored$score, feedback_scored$rating)
[1] 0.6424695

A correlation of about 0.64 means the two agree fairly well, but far from perfectly. Let us see where the method goes wrong.

49.7 Problem 1, negation

Look at these comments, and the scores they got.

feedback_scored |>
  filter(str_detect(comment, "\\bnot\\b")) |>
  distinct(comment, score) |>
  head(6)
# A tibble: 6 × 2
  comment                                                                  score
  <chr>                                                                    <int>
1 Food was not good.  Thank you, the operation was successful.                 3
2 Food was not good.  Treatment was done.                                      1
3 Toilets were not clean.  Treatment was done.                                 1
4 Staff was rude and careless.  Doctor was not available, very poor servi…    -2
5 Discharged after treatment.  Doctor was not available, very poor servic…     0
6 Toilets were not clean.  Very clean ward and good food.                      3

“Food was not good” got a positive score. The method saw the word good, found it in the dictionary as positive, and ignored the not before it. This is the biggest weakness of simple word counting. Let us see how often it happens. We use bigrams, from the last chapter, and look for a negative word followed by a dictionary word.

negations <- c("not", "no", "never", "without")

feedback |>
  unnest_tokens(bigram, comment, token = "ngrams", n = 2) |>
  separate_wider_delim(bigram, delim = " ", names = c("word1", "word2")) |>
  filter(word1 %in% negations) |>
  inner_join(bing, by = c(word2 = "word")) |>
  count(word1, word2, sentiment, sort = TRUE)
# A tibble: 6 × 4
  word1 word2     sentiment     n
  <chr> <chr>     <chr>     <int>
1 not   available positive     66
2 not   happy     positive     47
3 not   free      positive     45
4 not   good      positive     39
5 not   clean     positive     38
6 not   helpful   positive     35

Hundreds of positive words were really used in a negative sense. Not available, not happy, not free, not good, not clean, not helpful.

49.7.1 Fixing negation

A simple fix is to look at the word just before each dictionary word. If it is a negation, we flip the sign. lag() gives the previous word within each comment.

feedback_words_fixed <- feedback |>
  unnest_tokens(word, comment) |>
  mutate(previous = lag(word), .by = feedback_id) |>
  inner_join(bing, by = "word") |>
  mutate(value = if_else(sentiment == "positive", 1, -1),
         value = if_else(previous %in% negations, -value, value))

feedback_words_fixed |>
  filter(previous %in% negations) |>
  select(feedback_id, previous, word, sentiment, value) |>
  head(5)
# A tibble: 5 × 5
  feedback_id previous word  sentiment value
  <chr>       <chr>    <chr> <chr>     <dbl>
1 FB0001      not      good  positive     -1
2 FB0008      not      happy positive     -1
3 FB0010      not      happy positive     -1
4 FB0014      not      good  positive     -1
5 FB0020      not      clean positive     -1

Note that we added the previous column before joining with the dictionary. If we joined first, the words not in the dictionary, like not itself, would already have been dropped.

Now the score of a comment is simply the sum of these values.

score_comments <- function(word_values) {
  feedback |>
    left_join(word_values |> summarise(score = sum(value), .by = feedback_id),
              by = "feedback_id") |>
    mutate(score = replace_na(score, 0))
}

feedback_fixed <- score_comments(feedback_words_fixed)

cor(feedback_fixed$score, feedback_fixed$rating)
[1] 0.7814317

We wrote a small function, score_comments(), because we will use the same steps again. Custom functions were covered in Section 3.1. The correlation with the star rating has improved to 0.78.

49.8 Problem 2, words the dictionary does not know

A general dictionary is built from product reviews, news and the like. It does not know the language of every field. Let us see which common words in our comments are not in the dictionary.

feedback |>
  unnest_tokens(word, comment) |>
  anti_join(stop_words, by = "word") |>
  anti_join(bing, by = "word") |>
  count(word, sort = TRUE) |>
  head(25)
# A tibble: 25 × 2
   word            n
   <chr>       <int>
 1 treatment     394
 2 staff         357
 3 operation     309
 4 doctor        247
 5 ward          191
 6 food          185
 7 hospital      182
 8 caring        147
 9 doctors       147
10 experienced   146
# ℹ 15 more rows

Most of these are neutral, like treatment, staff and ward. But some clearly carry feeling in this context. Caring, recovered and experienced are positive. Forced, pay and wait are negative. In a free health scheme, pay is a strongly negative word, because patients should not have to pay at all.

So we add our own words to the dictionary. bind_rows() adds them below the Bing words.

my_words <- tribble(
  ~word,         ~sentiment,
  "caring",      "positive",
  "recovered",   "positive",
  "experienced", "positive",
  "explained",   "positive",
  "forced",      "negative",
  "pay",         "negative",
  "wait",        "negative",
  "nobody",      "negative"
)

my_dictionary <- bind_rows(bing, my_words)

Let us score the comments again, with negation handling and the extended dictionary.

feedback_final <- feedback |>
  unnest_tokens(word, comment) |>
  mutate(previous = lag(word), .by = feedback_id) |>
  inner_join(my_dictionary, by = "word") |>
  mutate(value = if_else(sentiment == "positive", 1, -1),
         value = if_else(previous %in% negations, -value, value)) |>
  score_comments()

cor(feedback_final$score, feedback_final$rating)
[1] 0.8184673

The agreement with the star rating has improved again, to 0.82. Each improvement came from understanding why the method went wrong, and fixing that specific problem.

TipAudit tip

Always check a sentiment method against something you trust, before relying on it. Here we had star ratings. Elsewhere, you can read and score 50 to 100 comments yourself, and compare with the method. If the method disagrees often, find out why, as we did, rather than accepting its numbers.

49.9 Comparing hospitals

Now let us use the scores. The simplest summary is the average score of each hospital, and the share of its comments that are negative.

            pct_negative = 100 * mean(score < 0),

WarningCaution, small numbers

An average built on ten comments is not reliable. Two hospitals with very different true quality can easily swap places by chance. Before ranking units by sentiment, either set a minimum number of comments, or compare groups of units, which gives larger numbers, as we do next.

49.9.1 Comparing groups

The earlier chapters gave us a group of six hospitals with unusual claims. Let us compare their feedback with that of all other hospitals together.

camp_hospitals <- c("H063", "H066", "H070", "H071", "H098", "H116")  # from the k-means chapter

feedback_final |>
  mutate(group = if_else(hospital_id %in% camp_hospitals,
                         "Six flagged hospitals", "All other hospitals")) |>
  summarise(comments     = n(),
            avg_score    = mean(score),
            pct_negative = 100 * mean(score < 0),
            avg_rating   = mean(rating),
            .by = group) |>
  mutate(across(where(is.double), \(x) round(x, 2)))
# A tibble: 2 × 5
  group                 comments avg_score pct_negative avg_rating
  <chr>                    <int>     <dbl>        <dbl>      <dbl>
1 All other hospitals       1424      1.01         23.0       3.62
2 Six flagged hospitals       76     -0.96         67.1       2.47

With the comments pooled, the difference is clear. Patients of the six flagged hospitals are far more often negative, and give lower star ratings. This is a third, independent source of evidence, after the claims data and the grievances, pointing to the same hospitals.

49.10 Beyond positive and negative, a red-flag dictionary

Sentiment tells us how people feel. For audit, it is often more useful to know what specific problems they mention. We can use the same dictionary method with our own list of red-flag words, which point to possible wrongdoing rather than just unhappiness.

red_flags <- c("forced", "pay", "paid", "money", "cash")

feedback |>
  unnest_tokens(word, comment) |>
  filter(word %in% red_flags) |>
  mutate(group = if_else(hospital_id %in% camp_hospitals,
                         "Six flagged hospitals", "All other hospitals")) |>
  count(group, word) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = word, values_from = n, values_fill = 0)
# A tibble: 2 × 3
  group                 forced   pay
  <chr>                  <int> <int>
1 All other hospitals       46    36
2 Six flagged hospitals      8     9

At first sight, the other hospitals seem to have more such comments. But this is a trap. There are many more comments about the other hospitals, so they will have more of almost everything. A fair comparison needs a rate, for example red-flag comments per 100 comments.

feedback |>
  mutate(group = if_else(hospital_id %in% camp_hospitals,
                         "Six flagged hospitals", "All other hospitals"),
         has_red_flag = str_detect(str_to_lower(comment),
                                   paste0("\\b(", paste(red_flags, collapse = "|"), ")\\b"))) |>
  summarise(comments = n(),
            red_flag_comments = sum(has_red_flag),
            per_100_comments = round(100 * mean(has_red_flag), 1),
            .by = group)
# A tibble: 2 × 4
  group                 comments red_flag_comments per_100_comments
  <chr>                    <int>             <int>            <dbl>
1 All other hospitals       1424                81              5.7
2 Six flagged hospitals       76                17             22.4

Now the picture is the right way round. Comments about the six flagged hospitals mention being forced or having to pay at about four times the rate of comments about other hospitals. Words like forced are much more important for an auditor than a general negative word like bad.

WarningCompare rates, not counts

Whenever groups differ in size, compare rates or percentages, not raw counts. A large district will have more complaints than a small one, simply because it is large. This simple point is behind many wrong conclusions.

TipAudit tip

Build a red-flag dictionary for each audit, with the help of people who know the sector. In a health scheme, it may include words about forced or unnecessary surgery, payments, and agents. In a PDS audit, it may include words about short weighing, refusal of ration, or poor quality grain. Such a dictionary is easy to explain in an audit report, and easy for the auditee to verify.

49.11 Audit use case, feedback that is too good to be true

So far we looked for units with bad feedback. But very good feedback can also be a warning sign. When feedback is collected by the staff of the unit being assessed, some of it may be written by the staff themselves. Such feedback tends to be glowing, uniform, and copied from a few sentences.

Let us take feedback collected through a mobile app from beneficiaries of 25 Fair Price Shops under the Public Distribution System. Each beneficiary writes a short comment and gives a rating. Genuine comments vary. Some praise the shop, many complain about short weighing, poor quality grain, or the shop being closed. In two shops, FPS-07 and FPS-19, we have planted feedback filled in by the shop staff, mostly copies of three sentences with 5 stars.

Code to create the ration shop feedback
set.seed(909)
positive_bits <- c("Ration received on time.", "Dealer behaviour is good.", "Grain quality was fine.",
                   "Full quantity given.", "Shop opens regularly.")
negative_bits <- c("Less quantity given than entitled.", "Shop was closed on the notified day.",
                   "Grain was of poor quality with stones.", "Dealer asked extra money.",
                   "Told to come again next week.", "Weighing machine is not working.")
neutral_bits  <- c("Took ration for the family.", "Came with my ration card.", "Collected wheat and rice.")

genuine_comment <- function() {
  k <- sample(1:2, 1)
  mood <- sample(c("pos", "neg", "mix"), 1, prob = c(0.4, 0.35, 0.25))
  bits <- switch(mood,
                 pos = sample(positive_bits, k),
                 neg = sample(negative_bits, k),
                 mix = c(sample(positive_bits, 1), sample(negative_bits, 1)))
  rating <- switch(mood, pos = sample(4:5, 1), neg = sample(1:2, 1), mix = sample(2:4, 1))
  # Each beneficiary also mentions what they collected, in their own way
  detail <- sprintf(sample(c("Got %d kg wheat and %d kg rice.", "Collected %d kg wheat, %d kg rice.",
                             "Family of %d, got rice %d kg."), 1),
                    sample(3:35, 1), sample(3:35, 1))
  tibble(comment = paste(c(sample(neutral_bits, 1), detail, bits), collapse = " "), rating = rating)
}

shops <- sprintf("FPS-%02d", 1:25)
fps_feedback <- map_dfr(shops, function(s) {
  map_dfr(1:sample(35:60, 1), \(i) genuine_comment()) |> mutate(shop = s, .before = 1)
})

# Planted. Staff-written feedback in two shops
templates <- c("Very good service, ration received on time, thank you.",

staff_written <- sample(staff_written, round(0.65 * length(staff_written)))
fps_feedback$comment[staff_written] <- sample(templates, length(staff_written), TRUE)
fps_feedback$rating[staff_written]  <- 5

fps_feedback <- fps_feedback |> mutate(feedback_id = row_number(), .before = 1)

First, let us score the comments with the Bing dictionary, handling negation as before, and rank the shops by their average score.

fps_scores <- fps_feedback |>
  unnest_tokens(word, comment, drop = FALSE) |>
  mutate(previous = lag(word), .by = feedback_id) |>
  inner_join(bing, by = "word") |>
  mutate(value = if_else(sentiment == "positive", 1, -1),
         value = if_else(previous %in% negations, -value, value)) |>
  summarise(score = sum(value), .by = feedback_id)

fps_scored <- fps_feedback |>
  left_join(fps_scores, by = "feedback_id") |>
  mutate(score = replace_na(score, 0))

3 FPS-01       46            0            17.4      0.28
4 FPS-02       60            0            18.3      0.28
5 FPS-03       46            0            26.1      0.13

In the two shops, most of the comments are copies of each other, and most ratings are 5 stars. In the other shops, not a single comment is a copy of another. Genuine beneficiaries write in their own words, mention their own quantities, and complain about different things. In real data, very short comments like “good” or “ok” will repeat by chance, so compare a unit with its peers rather than expecting zero duplicates.

TipAudit tip

Look at how people write, not only what they write. Many identical comments, uniform top ratings, and unusually high sentiment in one unit together suggest that the feedback was not written by the beneficiaries. Verify by contacting a sample of the beneficiaries whose names appear against those comments. The same check works for inspection reports, monitoring notes, and site visit remarks copied from one report to the next.

49.12 Limitations

WarningWhat dictionary methods cannot do
  • Sarcasm and irony. “Great service, waited only six hours!” will be scored as positive.
  • Context. Free is positive in “treatment was free”, but has nothing to do with sentiment in “free health camp”.
  • Mixed comments. A comment praising the doctor and complaining about the food gets a score near zero, which hides both points.
  • Other languages. Words in Hindi or other languages, and mixed-language text, are not in English dictionaries.
  • Longer negations. Our fix handles “not good”, but not “not at all good” or “I would not say it was good”.

More advanced methods, based on machine learning and large language models, handle some of these better. But they are harder to explain, and their errors are harder to spot. For audit work, a simple, transparent method that you have checked carefully is often the better choice.

49.13 A suggested workflow

  1. Read a sample of the text to understand how people write about the subject.
  2. Choose a dictionary. Start with Bing, which needs no download.
  3. Tokenise, join with the dictionary, and score each document.
  4. Check the scores against something you trust, such as ratings or your own reading of a sample.
  5. Fix the main problems, especially negation and missing domain words.
  6. Summarise by unit or group, with enough documents behind each number.
  7. Add a red-flag dictionary for the specific risks of the audit.
  8. Read the actual comments behind every unit you flag.
ImportantRemember

A sentiment score is a summary of many opinions, not a fact. Use it to decide where to look. The actual comments, read in full, are the evidence.
