60  Reconciliation and Cross-verification

Last updated

23 October 2026

A district health society keeps its money in one bank account. Its cashier writes every cheque, every e-payment and every receipt into the cash book. The bank keeps its own record of the same account, and sends it as a statement. The two records describe the same money, so at the end of the year they should agree. They seldom do. A cheque written on 28 March reaches the bank in April. The bank deducts its charges without telling anyone. A clerk types ₹54,320 where the cheque says ₹45,320. And once in a while, money leaves the account on a cheque that the cash book has never heard of.

Matching two such records is called reconciliation. It looks simple. Earlier we learnt to join two tables on a common key (Chapter 16). But a cash book and a bank statement have no common key. The cash book writes “CHQ 004512” and the bank writes “4512”. The amounts differ by a rupee because one side rounded. The dates differ by a few days because cheques take time to clear. One credit in the bank settles three bills that the cashier entered separately. An exact join handles only the tidy part of the problem.

The same task appears all over Indian audit, under the wider name of cross-verification. We check one record against an independent record that should agree with it. The input tax credit claimed by a business is checked against what its suppliers reported. A department’s expenditure is checked against the treasury’s figures. A scheme’s list of payments is checked against the bank’s list of credits. When two independent sources agree, we gain real assurance. When they disagree, the difference is often where the problem lies.

In this chapter we first learn the ideas behind a reconciliation. We then match a cash book with a bank statement in five steps, each step removing what it has matched. We study what is left, age it and value it, and present the familiar bank reconciliation statement. A second use case checks input tax credit against the suppliers’ returns, and a short third example compares a department’s figures with the treasury’s. Chapter 59, the previous chapter, checked that a ledger is complete before testing it. The same checks apply here, and we do not repeat them.

60.1 The idea of reconciliation

When we put two records of the same money side by side, every item falls into one of three groups.

  • Matched items. The item is in both records, with the same amount. A cheque of ₹25,000 written on 3 June and paid by the bank on 5 June is matched.
  • Timing differences. The item is in one record and will reach the other soon, without anyone doing anything. A cheque written on 30 March and paid by the bank on 4 April is an unpresented cheque at 31 March. Money deposited on 31 March and credited on 2 April is a deposit in transit. Timing differences clear by themselves.
  • Permanent differences. The item is in one record and will never reach the other unless someone acts. Bank charges that the office has not entered in its cash book stay missing until the office enters them. So do typing errors, cheques that were cancelled but never reversed, and irregular items such as an unauthorised withdrawal.

Every difference must be explained, and no item should stay in the reconciliation for long. An unpresented cheque of last week is normal. An unpresented cheque of last July is not. Banks do not pay a cheque presented more than three months after its date, so such a cheque is stale. It should be cancelled and the amount written back in the cash book. Very old reconciling items are a classic hiding place. A cheque that was never meant to be paid, or a receipt that never reached the bank, can sit in the reconciliation for years, explained each month as “timing”. So we always age the reconciling items, and look hardest at the old ones.

60.2 Our data, a cash book and a bank statement

Our auditee is the District Health Society of an imaginary district, Keshavnagar. It receives grants from the State Health Society every quarter, collects fees and rents, and pays suppliers, contractors and staff. All of this passes through one savings bank account. We have received three things for 2024-25.

  • cash_book is the bank column of the society’s cash book. Each row has an id cb_id, the date, the particulars, a reference ref, and the amount as a receipt or a payment. Cheques are written “CHQ 004512”. E-payments carry the society’s own advice number, such as “EP/24-25/0001”, and receipts the receipt number.
  • bank is the bank statement for the same year, with an id bs_id, the date, the bank’s narration, the cheque number ref for cheques, the withdrawal or deposit, and the running balance.
  • bank_apr is the bank statement for April 2025, the month after the year end.

The reconciliation at 31 March 2024 had no outstanding items, so the cash book and the bank both opened the year with the same balance, opening. Most transactions are routine. Cheques take one to five days to clear, and some take longer. E-payments are debited the same day or within two days. Cheques deposited take one to three days to be credited. Twelve shops in the hospital complex each pay the same rent of ₹15,000 every month. These are the planted cases.

  1. Unpresented cheques. Cheques written in the last week of March reach the bank in April.
  2. Deposits in transit. Cheques deposited on the last days of March are credited in April.
  3. Bank charges and interest not booked. The society entered the bank’s charges and interest until December. It did not enter those of January to March.
  4. A transposition error. A cheque of ₹45,320 is entered in the cash book as ₹54,320.
  5. A cheque that never reached the bank. A large cheque to a contractor in November 2024 was never presented.
  6. A stale cheque. A small cheque of July 2024 is still unpresented at the year end.
  7. A debit in the bank that is not in the cash book. Cash was withdrawn on a “self” cheque from the society’s own cheque book. The cash book has no entry for it.
  8. One credit for several receipts. One party paid three bills by a single transfer, and the cashier entered three receipts. Another party paid two bills at once.
  9. Cheque numbers written differently. The cash book writes “CHQ 004512”, the bank “4512”. Four cheque numbers were also typed wrongly in the cash book.

There is also genuine noise. E-payments are rounded to the rupee, while the cash book sometimes shows paise. Dates differ by a few days in normal clearing.

TipAudit tip, start with the auditee’s own reconciliation

Every office that keeps a bank account must reconcile it, usually every month. So before we match anything, we ask for the office’s own reconciliation statements for the year, with their lists of reconciling items. Then we test them rather than redo them blindly. Does the statement start from the cash book balance and end at the bank balance? Do the items listed really exist in the records? Are the same items carried month after month? Has anything old quietly disappeared without an entry? The matching in this chapter is the independent check. It is most useful when the office has no reconciliation, when its reconciliation does not close, or when the same items keep appearing.

Get the statement from the bank, or download it in our presence, rather than accept a copy from the office. Altered statements are not unknown. Record a hash of each file received, as in Section 8.6. If the statement comes as a PDF, Section 12.2 shows how to read it into R and check its running balance.

60.3 Matching step by step

60.3.1 Preparing the two sides

The two records describe the same events in different words. A receipt in the cash book is a deposit in the bank, and a payment is a withdrawal. So we first bring both to one shape. Each item gets a direction, “in” or “out”, and a positive amount. We also give the columns of each side a prefix, cb_ or bs_, so that we can tell them apart after a join.

The cheque number needs most care. The cash book writes “CHQ 004512”, the bank “4512”. An exact join on these finds nothing. Let us see how to clean them on a tiny example.

60.3.4 Step 3, one item, one match

An item may be matched only once. If a bank credit is used to explain two cash book entries, one of those entries is really unmatched, and we have hidden a difference. So from the candidates we must choose one pair for each item. We use a simple rule, and we write it down.

  1. Prefer the pair with the closest dates.
  2. Among those, prefer the smallest difference in amount.
  3. Among those, prefer the pair whose party names agree.

We sort the candidates by these rules and walk down the list. We take a pair only if neither of its items has been taken already. This is called a greedy match. Let us see it on two rents.

t_cand <- tibble(cb_id    = c("C1", "C1", "C2", "C2"),
                 bs_id    = c("B1", "B2", "B1", "B2"),
                 day_gap  = c(0, 3, 2, 1),
                 amt_diff = 0,
                 same_party = TRUE)

one_to_one <- function(cand) {
  cand <- arrange(cand, day_gap, abs(amt_diff), desc(same_party), cb_id, bs_id)
  used_cb <- character(0)
  used_bs <- character(0)
  keep <- logical(nrow(cand))
  for (i in seq_len(nrow(cand))) {
    if (!(cand$cb_id[i] %in% used_cb) && !(cand$bs_id[i] %in% used_bs)) {
      keep[i] <- TRUE
      used_cb <- c(used_cb, cand$cb_id[i])
      used_bs <- c(used_bs, cand$bs_id[i])
    }
  }
  cand[keep, ]
}

find_combo <- function(target, amounts, max_size = 3, tolerance = 1) {
  if (length(amounts) < 2) return(NULL)
  for (k in 2:min(max_size, length(amounts))) {
    pos <- combn(length(amounts), k)
    sums <- colSums(matrix(amounts[pos], nrow = k))
    hit <- which(abs(sums - target) <= tolerance)
    if (length(hit) > 0) return(pos[, hit[1]])
  }
  NULL
}

60.3.6 Step 5, references typed wrongly

Some cheques are still unmatched on both sides. A cheque matches in step 1 only if its number is typed correctly. WarningCaution, a match is not proof

A matched pair may still be wrong. The tolerance and the window let us pair items that are close, and two unrelated items can be close by chance. Two errors can also cancel. A payment left out of the cash book and a receipt left out in the same week, of similar amounts, leave the totals correct. A match says only that the two records agree on that item. It does not say that the payment was proper.

The tolerance, the window and the size of combinations are our choices, and different choices give different results. Choose them from the data, as we chose the window from the clearing times in step 1. Keep them as small as the data allows. State them in the working papers, with the number of items matched at each step, so that a reviewer can repeat the work.

60.4.3 The reconciliation statement

We can now present the statement in the familiar form. It starts from the balance as per the cash book. It adds the items that make the bank balance higher and takes away those that make it lower. It should end at the balance as per the bank.

Each kind of item moves the bank balance in a known direction. A payment in the cash book that the bank has not yet made leaves more money in the bank, so it is added. A deposit the bank has not yet credited is taken away. Interest credited by the bank only is added, and charges and withdrawals by the bank only are taken away. The transposition error made the cash book payment larger than the bank’s, so the difference is added. The rounding differences are added or taken away according to their net sign.

60.4.4 What the auditor does next

  1. The self cheque. Get the cheque from the bank, with its image and the signatures on it. Check the cheque issue register and the counterfoil. Find out who withdrew the cash, and whether it was ever brought into the cash book. Treat it as a possible misappropriation until it is explained, and report it at once to the head of the office.
  2. The cheque never presented. Ask for the bill, the sanction and the contractor’s acknowledgement. Ask the contractor, in writing, whether the cheque was received. If it was cancelled, see that the cash book is reversed. If no work was done for it, the payment was not real.
  3. The stale cheque. See that it is cancelled and written back, and that the supplier is paid afresh if still owed.
  4. Charges and interest. See that they are entered, and that the interest is dealt with as the scheme’s instructions require.
  5. The errors. See that the transposition and the four wrong cheque numbers are corrected in the cash book.
  6. The controls. Ask why the office’s own reconciliations did not bring out these items. A monthly reconciliation done properly would have shown the self cheque in January.

60.5 Audit use case, input tax credit against suppliers’ returns

Under GST, a business pays tax on its sales. It may reduce that tax by the tax it has already paid on its purchases. This reduction is called input tax credit. Each month the business files a summary return, GSTR-3B, in which it pays its tax and claims its credit. Its suppliers, in their own returns, report every invoice they issued to it. From these reports the GST system prepares for the business a monthly statement, GSTR-2B, listing the invoices on which credit is available.

So two records exist. The business’s purchase register shows the credit it claimed, invoice by invoice. The GSTR-2B shows the invoices its suppliers reported. Broadly, credit claimed on an invoice that no supplier reported is not supported. It is the main risk, because a fake invoice from a supplier who files nothing gives credit that was never paid as tax by anyone. The rules on such claims have changed over time and have exceptions, so the treatment of each difference should be checked against the GST law in force. Our task is the reconciliation itself.

Our business is an imaginary food processing company, Keshav Agro Foods Private Limited. Its purchase register purchases has one row per invoice claimed in 2024-25. gstr2b has one row per invoice reported by its suppliers. The firm names and GSTINs are made up, but each GSTIN is built with a correct check character, using the function of Section 21.11. These cases are planted.

  1. Credit on invoices that suppliers never reported. All the invoices of one supplier, and six invoices of other suppliers.
  2. Invoice numbers written differently. Three suppliers write “INV-0012” in their returns, where the register has “INV/24-25/0012”.
  3. Small rounding differences. The company rounded the tax on some invoices to the rupee.
  4. An invoice claimed twice. The same invoice appears in two months of the register.
  5. A GSTIN that fails the check. One invoice is entered under a GSTIN with a typing error.

Some invoices were also reported late by their suppliers, a month after the invoice.

gst_chars <- c(0:9, LETTERS)

gstin_check_char <- function(first14) {
  v <- match(strsplit(first14, "")[[1]], gst_chars) - 1
  p <- v * rep(c(1, 2), 7)
  total <- sum(p %/% 36 + p %% 36)
  gst_chars[((36 - total %% 36) %% 36) + 1]
}

gstin_valid <- function(x) {
  x <- str_to_upper(str_remove_all(x, "\\s"))
  map_lgl(x, function(g) {
    if (is.na(g) || !str_detect(g, "^[0-9]{2}[A-Z]{5}[0-9]{4}[A-Z][1-9A-Z]Z[0-9A-Z]$")) {
      return(FALSE)
    }
    gstin_check_char(str_sub(g, 1, 14)) == str_sub(g, 15, 15)
  })
}

60.5.3 What the auditor does next

  1. Quantify the unsupported credit. Credit on invoices never reported, ₹6.7 lakh, and the second claim of the duplicate invoice, ₹8,305.08. Check each against the GST law in force, including any conditions that allow credit in special cases.
  2. Look at the supplier that reported nothing. Check its registration status, its returns and its tax payments on the GST system. Ask the company for the purchase orders, goods received notes, transport documents and payment proofs for its invoices.
  3. Follow up the single invoices. Ask whether the company has taken up the omissions with its suppliers, and whether the invoices were reported later.
  4. Ignore what is explained. Rounding differences and invoices reported a month late are timing or trivial. Record them, but do not report them.
  5. Report data errors as such. The wrong GSTIN and the different invoice formats are weaknesses in the company’s records, not losses of revenue.

60.6 Cross-verification of aggregates, department and treasury

Sometimes we do not have item-level data on both sides, or we do not need it. A department keeps its own record of expenditure. The treasury, which passes and pays the bills, keeps another. The two should agree each month for each head of account, and departments are usually required to reconcile their figures with the treasury’s. Here the comparison is of totals, by month and head.

60.7 A suggested workflow

  1. Ask for the auditee’s own reconciliations and test them first. Note old items and balancing figures.
  2. Get both records independently, with opening and closing balances, and record a hash of each file. Check that each is complete.
  3. Bring both sides to one shape. Direction, positive amount, cleaned references, and party names in one form.
  4. Match exact pairs on reference and amount, with na_matches = "never" and relationship = "one-to-one". Use the matched pairs to learn the usual delay.
  5. Match on amount and date with an inequality join, using a tolerance and window chosen from the data.
  6. Resolve one to one, by the closest date, then the smallest difference, then the same party, so that no item is used twice.
  7. Search small combinations of items within a party and a window, for one item that settles several.
  8. Match references typed wrongly with a string distance, keeping the amount equal.
  9. Classify, age and value what is left. Timing or permanent, and how old. Test the timing items against the next statement.
  10. Present the statement and check that it closes. Follow up the permanent and old items, the serious ones first.
ImportantRemember
  • Reconciliation explains every difference between two records of the same money. Cross-verification checks a record against an independent one.
  • Timing differences clear by themselves. Permanent differences need an entry or an investigation. Old items in a reconciliation are where problems hide.
  • Match in steps, from the surest to the weakest, and remove matches at each step. Never let one item match twice.
  • A tolerance and a window are choices. Base them on the data, keep them small, and write them down.
  • A match is not proof that a transaction is proper, and a statement that closes is not the end of the work.
  • The most serious items are often the smallest in number, such as one debit in the bank that the books do not know about.
